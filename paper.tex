\begin{abstract}
Large language models answer legal questions fluently but unreliably, fabricating case names, misquoting statutory provisions, and citing repealed law. Measured hallucination rates on verifiable case-law questions range from 58\% to 88\% even at temperature zero, and models are poorly calibrated about their own legal errors. Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG) reduces but does not eliminate this failure: across published Indian legal RAG systems, hallucination falls from roughly 30\% for ungrounded generation to approximately 6\% for the most sophisticated grounded architecture, but never reaches zero, because every surveyed system controls hallucination \emph{statistically} — through adversarial evaluators, confidence weighting, or partly manual expert review. This paper proposes \textit{Is It Legal?}, a RAG engine for Indian law whose central contribution is a deterministic, model-free citation verifier that makes citation precision equal to unity \emph{by construction}: every citation marker must resolve to a retrieved chunk, every quoted span must string-match its source above a fixed similarity threshold, and every cited provision is checked against repeal and amendment metadata. Three further contributions address documented gaps in the literature: a unified corpus spanning statutes and judgments with a bidirectional mapping across the 2023 Indian criminal-law recodification (IPC$\rightarrow$BNS, CrPC$\rightarrow$BNSS, Evidence Act$\rightarrow$BSA); an externally calibrated abstention gate that reports insufficient authority rather than guessing; and an evaluation protocol grounded in community benchmarks rather than bespoke metrics. We present the system architecture, corpus construction pipeline, verifier algorithm, and a complete evaluation design. We further report measurements from a partial implementation. The citation normaliser resolves 48/48 held-out surface forms with zero false positives, and the verifier detects 13/13 planted citation faults with no false alarms on clean inputs. Because strip-only verification buys precision with coverage, we introduce evidence-bounded repair, which recovers 16.7 percentage points of coverage on faulty answers while an independent post-hoc audit confirms zero invariant violations. A diagnostic on the 2023 recodification finds that lexical retrieval ranks the in-force provision above its repealed predecessor in only 33.3\% of cases --- a failure conventional retrieval metrics do not expose --- whereas the mapping layer annotates the superseding provision in 100\% of them. To narrow the architecture's central blind spot --- a real, in-force, correctly quoted provision attached to an unrelated claim passes every well-formedness check --- we add a deterministic IDF-weighted support screen, which flags 81.8\% of incorrect attributions while flagging no correct claim. We characterise where it fails: correct and incorrect attributions are cleanly separable when the wrong provision is unrelated (margin $+0.52$) but overlap when it is topically adjacent (margin $-0.09$), a boundary we argue is principled rather than incidental, since adjacent provisions differ in grammatical role rather than vocabulary. We further implement the calibrated abstention gate as a threshold on an IDF-normalised retrieval score, tuned on a development split and reported on a held-out one; it answers every held-out answerable query and abstains on every held-out unanswerable one, and we report that choosing the signal by development-set performance would instead have selected raw BM25 top-1, which generalises worse. Every architectural stage now exists in code and is wired into a complete pipeline: BM25 and dense retrieval, Reciprocal Rank Fusion, cross-encoder reranking, the confidence gate, a generator interface, and the verifier. To make retrieval measurement meaningful we replace the saturated 35-provision corpus with a real, sourced, 3{,}263-provision corpus of verbatim statutory text spanning all ten Central Acts the recodification concerns, scraped and validated with the same adversarial methodology applied throughout this paper across five rounds of audit (eleven parser defects found and fixed, one of them correcting this paper's own prior conclusion that a merged section was a source gap rather than a parser defect, the fourth round a systematic sweep of the corpus itself rather than reactive probing, and the fifth round a repeat of that sweep finding four further defects it had missed the first time); BM25 Recall@1 falls from a meaningless 1.000 to 0.375, below the 0.49--0.60 range this paper had cited from comparable corpora as an expectation rather than a measurement. Hybrid fusion outperforms either retrieval arm alone (MRR@10 0.680 against 0.518 for BM25 and 0.652 for dense), and reranking improves nDCG substantially (+0.038) while barely moving MRR (+0.005) or Recall@1 (unchanged), a milder version of the divergence this paper's methodology was designed to catch: not opposite-signed, but concentrated almost entirely in ranking quality among already-retrieved results rather than in promoting the single best result to rank 1. Using the same generating model as this research session in place of an unavailable API credential, we further run real, unscripted generated answers -- not scripted probes -- through the unmodified verifier for the first time: citation resolution holds at 1.000 pre- and post-verification in both an ungrounded and a retrieval-grounded condition, while repair automatically upgraded nine superseded citations with transparent annotations, one unmapped repealed citation was correctly annotated rather than silently dropped or resolved, and one genuine quotation error made while composing a grounded answer was caught and stripped -- a demonstration of the wiring under genuine text, stated plainly as neither blinded nor representative of hallucination rates in general. The dense-retrieval ablation itself is a negative result: a domain-adapted legal encoder (InLegalBERT) underperforms both a general-purpose retrieval-tuned encoder and plain BM25, evidence that domain-adapted pre-training without a retrieval-specific fine-tuning objective does not transfer to retrieval quality. The pipeline has since been run end to end against a real, independently invoked generator (Groq, openai/gpt-oss-20b) with no visibility into this project's internals: applied to the full sixteen-query gold set, the real-corpus confidence gate passes ten and correctly refuses all sixteen deliberately unanswerable queries; across the ten generated answers, pre- and post-verifier citation resolution both hold at 1.000; and the quotation check fires eight times, an order of magnitude more than the one instance seen in this paper's earlier, self-composed round, correctly stripping every one and dropping mean coverage to 0.930 -- the strongest evidence yet that the mechanism does real work rather than passing because it was rarely tested. What remains is stated exactly rather than elided: the query set is still this project's own, authored before retrieval was tuned to bound but not remove that authorship, so this is an advance in generator independence, not yet in query-set independence; running the full ablation on a large, externally-sourced adversarial set remains this project's single largest remaining piece of work. We also report negative findings that changed the design: character-level fuzzy matching, the obvious choice for quotation verification, scores a meaning-inverting single-word substitution at 95.9\% and admits it, where token-level matching rejects it; and twelve parser defects found by four rounds of adversarial audit --- most seriously, that enumerated references such as ``sections 302 and 304 of the IPC'' extracted no citations at all, leaving a fabricated section concealed in a list invisible to the existence check. Because the guarantee is void wherever the parser fails to extract, we treat these as holes in the guarantee rather than gaps in coverage: a second audit conducted after the first was believed complete found six further such holes, including section numbers wrapped in markdown emphasis and separated by a slash, both of which generators emit routinely. Repairing them changed no previously reported number in either round, because the probe sets had by coincidence avoided every broken construct: a stable score across a correctness-changing edit is evidence of an insufficiently adversarial evaluation, not of robustness. A third round, found not by auditing the parser but by inspecting a genuinely generated live answer during this project's own demo build, uncovered two further defects, one of them the most severe found in any round: a hyphenated citation (``Section 65-B'', this paper's own running example for the Evidence Act's electronic-records provision) silently truncated and validated against a real but unrelated shorter provision rather than going unchecked --- a false anchor, not a blind spot, and one observable only against the real corpus, since the 35-provision development corpus has no same-numeral collision pair to expose it. A fourth round, from the live-generation experiment itself rather than either the demo or deliberate probing, found one further defect: an act name immediately followed by a parenthesised section reference, with no comma or ``of the'' between them, extracted no citation at all on a real, existing provision the independently invoked model actually cited.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Legal research in India is slow, expensive, and error-prone. A practitioner or layperson asking whether a particular act is lawful requires not merely an answer but \emph{citable authority} — the specific statutory section or judicial precedent that supports the conclusion, in a form that can be independently verified before it is relied upon.

Large language models (LLMs) appear to supply exactly this. They answer legal questions in fluent, confident, professionally registered prose. The difficulty is that a substantial fraction of those answers are wrong in a particularly dangerous way: the citations are fabricated. Dahl \textit{et al.} \cite{dahl2024} queried LLMs with verifiable questions about randomly sampled United States federal cases and found hallucination rates between 58\% (GPT-4) and 88\% (Llama-2), even at temperature zero. Two secondary findings of that study matter as much as the headline number. First, models are poorly calibrated regarding their own legal hallucinations — they cannot reliably flag when they are wrong. Second, in contra-factual setups they frequently accept and elaborate upon a user's false legal premise rather than correcting it.

Three features of the Indian legal context sharpen the problem:

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Exact-reference density.} Legal queries are entity-heavy. ``Section 138 of the Negotiable Instruments Act'' must match exactly; a semantically adjacent but numerically different section is not a partial success but a complete failure.
\item \textbf{Statutory volatility.} In July 2024 the Indian Penal Code, Code of Criminal Procedure, and Indian Evidence Act were replaced by the Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita (BNS), Bharatiya Nagarik Suraksha Sanhita (BNSS), and Bharatiya Sakshya Adhiniyam (BSA) respectively \cite{dixit2025}. Sections were rearranged and renumbered. Any system trained or indexed on the pre-2023 codes is now systematically misaligned with current law.
\item \textbf{Document length and structure.} Indian Supreme Court judgments routinely run to tens of thousands of words, with the operative \textit{ratio decidendi} often distant from the passage that best matches a query lexically.
\end{enumerate}

Retrieval-Augmented Generation \cite{lewis2020} is the natural response, supplying both provenance (answers traceable to retrieved evidence) and updatability (swapping the index updates the system's knowledge without retraining). Yet the evidence assembled in Section~\ref{sec:related} shows that grounding alone is insufficient. Reading published Indian legal RAG systems together, hallucination declines monotonically with architectural sophistication — approximately 30.6\% for ungrounded generation, 16.9\% for vanilla RAG, and 6.3\% for a graph-augmented adversarial architecture \cite{bindra2026} — but no system reaches zero. The reason is structural: in every case the control mechanism is statistical or human-in-the-loop, never a hard guarantee.

\subsection{Contributions}

This paper proposes a system positioned precisely at that residue. Our contributions are:

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{C1 — A deterministic citation verifier.} A model-free post-processing layer that enforces, by construction, that every citation marker resolves to a retrieved chunk, that every quoted span matches its source at token granularity above a calibrated threshold, and that every cited provision carries current repeal and amendment status. Citation precision is unity not as a target but as an invariant.
\item \textbf{C1b — Evidence-bounded repair.} Strip-only verification attains precision by deletion, at heavy cost to coverage. We introduce two repair rules that recover claims whose fault is recoverable from evidence already retrieved --- a misattributed marker, or a superseded reference with a recorded successor --- while leaving fabricated content unrepaired by design. Repair is admissible because each repaired sentence is re-verified by the same checks, so it cannot trade precision for coverage.
\item \textbf{C2 — A unified statute--judgment corpus with recodification mapping.} A bidirectional IPC$\leftrightarrow$BNS (and CrPC$\leftrightarrow$BNSS, IEA$\leftrightarrow$BSA) section mapping, with automatic transition annotations emitted in answers.
\item \textbf{C3 — A calibrated abstention gate.} An external retrieval-confidence threshold, tuned on a development split and evaluated on deliberately unanswerable queries, permitting the system to report insufficient authority.
\item \textbf{C1c — A deterministic support screen with a characterised failure boundary.} Checks V1--V4 establish that a citation is well-formed but not that it is \emph{apposite}: a real, in-force, correctly quoted provision attached to an unrelated claim passes all of them. We add an IDF-weighted lexical screen that detects topical mismatch without invoking a model. We show that it separates grossly mismatched attributions cleanly but \emph{fails} to separate topically adjacent ones, and we therefore deploy it as advisory rather than as a strip rule, at the threshold that keeps flagging precision at unity.
\item \textbf{C4 — A standardised, comparable evaluation protocol} combining community relevance judgments, reference-free RAG metrics, deterministic citation metrics, and adversarial false-premise probes.
\item \textbf{C5 — Index-time cross-reference resolution.} A statutory citation graph built by resolving intra-act references against their enclosing act at index time, with inbound traversal to recover provisions whose own text is too thin to be retrieved. This addresses a failure mode diagnosed but explicitly left to future work by Rustamov \textit{et al.} \cite{rustamov2025}.
\end{itemize}

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}

We organise the literature into five threads that jointly define the design space.

\subsection{Thread A: Foundations of Retrieval-Augmented Generation}

Lewis \textit{et al.} \cite{lewis2020} introduced RAG as a general recipe combining a parametric generator with a non-parametric dense index, establishing provenance and updatability as native properties. Karpukhin \textit{et al.} \cite{karpukhin2020} demonstrated with Dense Passage Retrieval (DPR) that a dual-encoder architecture trained with in-batch negatives substantially outperforms BM25 on open-domain retrieval. DPR's known weakness is directly consequential for law: dense retrievers underperform on queries dominated by rare exact tokens — precisely the section numbers and act names that dominate legal queries. This complementarity is the empirical root of the hybrid-retrieval consensus.

Sun \cite{sun2025} operationalises that consensus adaptively. The AH-RAG system fuses BM25 and dense (BGE-small/FAISS) scores using a per-query fusion weight computed from query complexity, an intent classifier, and historical feedback, then rescales the generator's attention over documents at decoding time according to a context-confidence estimate. On Natural Questions, Recall@20 rises from 52.4\% (BM25) to 67.7\%, a gain of 15.3 points over BM25 and 4.4 over DPR, at only 4.8\% additional end-to-end latency. Ablations attribute 1--3 recall points to dynamic fusion and 0.5--1.5 EM/F1 points to confidence control. Two findings transfer to our design: the optimal sparse/dense balance is query-dependent, and propagating retrieval confidence into generation measurably reduces hallucination.

\subsection{Thread B: Adaptive Retrieval and Long Documents}

Asai \textit{et al.} \cite{asai2023} train a language model to emit reflection tokens governing its own retrieval behaviour — whether to retrieve, whether a passage is relevant, whether output is supported, whether the response is useful — yielding gains in factuality and citation accuracy in long-form generation. Training reflection tokens is beyond our scope, but Self-RAG supplies the conceptual justification for two of our components: an explicit abstention decision and a post-generation support check. Our deterministic verifier can be read as a hard-coded, model-free analogue of Self-RAG's ``is this output supported?'' critique, trading learned flexibility for a guarantee.

Sarthi \textit{et al.} \cite{sarthi2024} address the mismatch between flat chunk retrieval and document-level questions. RAPTOR recursively embeds, clusters, and summarises chunks into a tree whose upper nodes hold progressively more abstractive summaries, improving accuracy on the QuALITY long-document benchmark by approximately 20 points absolute when coupled with GPT-4. The legal application is immediate and motivates our parent-document retrieval design.

\subsection{Thread C: Legal-Domain Language Models and Indian Resources}

Chalkidis \textit{et al.} \cite{chalkidis2020} released LEGAL-BERT, pre-trained on English-language legal text, showing consistent gains over generic BERT and demonstrating that sub-domain adaptation matters. Zheng \textit{et al.} \cite{zheng2021} asked \emph{when} domain pre-training helps, showing with CaseLawBERT that gains are largest precisely when the task is domain-specific and difficult.

Paul \textit{et al.} \cite{paul2023} extended this to India, pre-training InLegalBERT and InCaseLawBERT on approximately 5.4 million Indian court documents spanning 1950--2019, continuing from LegalBERT and CaseLawBERT checkpoints. InLegalBERT outperforms both its generic and foreign-legal-domain ancestors on legal statute identification, rhetorical-role segmentation, and judgment prediction. The implication is that Indian legal text is sufficiently distinct — in terminology, citation convention, drafting style, and register — that even \emph{legal-domain} foreign models are suboptimal.

For corpora and benchmarks, Malik \textit{et al.} \cite{malik2021} introduced ILDC, approximately 35{,}000 annotated Supreme Court judgments, together with the Court Judgment Prediction and Explanation task and a documented gap between model and expert explanations. Joshi \textit{et al.} \cite{joshi2024} consolidated the field with IL-TUR, spanning rhetorical role labelling, named-entity recognition, judgment prediction, bail prediction, summarisation, and — most relevantly for us — legal statute identification and prior case retrieval, with a public leaderboard. Their baselines show that no single model, including GPT-3.5 and GPT-4, performs well across tasks. The FIRE AILA track \cite{bhattacharya2019} remains the only Indian shared task constructed specifically for \emph{retrieval}, providing official relevance judgments for both precedent and statute retrieval.

On structure, Mamakas \textit{et al.} \cite{mamakas2022} modify LegalBERT into a Longformer-style sparse-attention model processing up to 8{,}192 tokens, outperforming truncation-based baselines. Together with rhetorical-role work on Indian judgments, this justifies structure-aware chunking with hierarchical context in preference to brute-force long-context encoding.

\subsection{Thread D: Applied Legal RAG Systems}

Six systems sit closest to our work. Table~\ref{tab:applied} summarises them.

Patil \textit{et al.} \cite{patil2025} built BNS Mitra, mapping informal incident descriptions to BNS sections. LLaMA-2 first rephrases the colloquial query into formal legal terminology; the rephrased query is embedded and matched against a FAISS index of BNS chunks produced by a recursive character splitter. On 500 queries with lawyer adjudication the system achieved 87\% section-recommendation accuracy, with RAG contributing a 12-point improvement over the non-RAG configuration. Accuracy was highest on straightforward offences and degraded on complex fact patterns where multiple sections plausibly apply. Their own qualitative examples show questionable mappings — a bodily-assault scenario routed to mischief provisions. Three lessons follow: query rewriting into legal register is cheap and effective; character-based chunking of statutes is a defect we correct with section-boundary chunking; and multi-section applicability is the hard case any serious evaluation must stratify for.

Dixit \textit{et al.} \cite{dixit2025} present Nyaya-Sathi, motivated explicitly by the recodification. Metadata-tagged BNS sections are embedded into ChromaDB; retrieved sections condition structured generation via an LLM API; and a post-processing stage checks generated citations against the database, discarding unverifiable content. They report Precision@1 of 0.85 against 0.60 without RAG, citation accuracy of 0.92 against 0.70, and hallucination reduced from 0.25 to 0.05. Critically, the authors state that the verification step involves human-expert confirmation, and they acknowledge limitations of statutes-only coverage, absence of case law and cross-references, and a small evaluation user base.

Bolla \textit{et al.} \cite{bolla2026} implement the architecture closest to our retrieval stack, applied to the Constitution of India: articles are dually indexed as InLegalBERT dense vectors and a BM25 lexical index, merged by Reciprocal Rank Fusion, reranked by a MiniLM cross-encoder, and summarised by an LLM. They report a hybrid F1 of 89.4\%, with hybrid retrieval outperforming both single-arm configurations across precision, recall, and F1 — a clean confirmation of the sparse-plus-dense-plus-rerank consensus on Indian legal text specifically. The system offers no abstention mechanism and reports no hallucination metrics.

Bindra \textit{et al.} \cite{bindra2026} contribute the most architecturally ambitious Indian system, indexing over 10{,}000 Supreme Court judgments plus the Constitution and CrPC both as dense vectors and as a citation graph, then performing constrained multi-hop traversal to recover doctrinally related precedents sharing no surface semantics with the query. A GAN-inspired adversarial module retrieves supporting and opposing arguments, with a discriminative evaluator penalising one-sided or unsupported output. Accuracy rises from 63\% to 77\%; groundedness from 0.43 (LLM-only) and 0.73 (vanilla RAG) to 0.95; precedent recall from 0.56 and 0.72 to 0.87; hallucination falls from 30.6\% and 16.9\% to 6.3\%; and citation accuracy rises from 1.8\% and 67.4\% to 96.1\%, at a latency cost of 2.5\,s to 15.3\,s. Their case study on arrest powers under Section 41 CrPC shows that only the graph-augmented configuration surfaced the constitutional dimension (Articles 21 and 22) alongside the statute. Three implications follow for us: the 1.8\% citation accuracy of ungrounded generation is a striking Indian-law confirmation of Dahl \textit{et al.}; citation-graph traversal is the strongest empirical case for relational structure; and the residual 6.3\% hallucination persists precisely because the controls are statistical rather than deterministic.

Rustamov \textit{et al.} \cite{rustamov2025} provide the methodologically cleanest study in this thread, on the Azerbaijani Tax Code — a different jurisdiction but a structurally identical problem. From 5{,}657 citizen question--answer pairs they retain 2{,}178 answerable purely from the Code's 240 articles, chunked at 300 tokens with 60-token overlap following an analysis of article length distributions (mean approximately 1{,}500 tokens, median 670). Benchmarking two sparse and three dense retrievers across six IR metrics, they find that the best single retriever (BGE-m3) reaches only 0.49 recall@100; weighted hybrid BM25+BGE-m3 lifts recall to 0.60; and cross-encoder reranking improves NDCG from 0.39 to 0.44 while \emph{decreasing} MRR from 0.60 to 0.39, failing to promote the first relevant document. Their error analysis is the paper's most valuable element: retrieval failures concentrate on articles that are not semantically self-contained because they merely cross-reference other articles, a dense-retrieval blind spot the authors argue requires knowledge-graph structure to resolve.

\subsection{Thread E: Hallucination Measurement and RAG Evaluation}

Dahl \textit{et al.} \cite{dahl2024}, discussed in Section~I, supply both the motivating measurement and a typology distinguishing fidelity to inputs, to training data, and to legal fact. Es \textit{et al.} \cite{es2023} introduced RAGAS, evaluating RAG pipelines without gold answers by decomposing quality into faithfulness, answer relevance, and context relevance, each scored via structured LLM prompting. RAGAS supplies three of our evaluation metrics. Its known limitation — that LLM-as-judge scores are themselves fallible and unsuited to guaranteeing zero hallucination — is precisely why our protocol pairs RAGAS (graded, diagnostic) with deterministic citation checks (binary, guaranteed).

\subsection{Convergent Findings}

Read together, five conclusions recur with sufficient independence across the surveyed corpus to be treated as established:

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Hybrid retrieval is a consensus, not a design choice.} Every study comparing sparse, dense, and hybrid configurations — AH-RAG on open-domain QA \cite{sun2025}, Rustamov \textit{et al.} on statutory text \cite{rustamov2025}, Constitutional Compass on Indian constitutional text \cite{bolla2026} — found hybrid superior. The two dense-only Indian systems exhibit exactly the failure modes that sparse signals address.
\item \textbf{Retrieval, not generation, is the recall bottleneck.} A 0.49 single-model recall ceiling \cite{rustamov2025} and the precedent-recall gains from graph traversal \cite{bindra2026} both locate the hard problem in \emph{finding} the correct authority.
\item \textbf{Grounding reduces but does not eliminate hallucination.} The progression 30.6\% $\rightarrow$ 16.9\% $\rightarrow$ 6.3\% \cite{bindra2026}, and 0.25 $\rightarrow$ 0.05 with partly manual verification \cite{dixit2025}, is consistent. No surveyed system reaches zero, and none implements a fully deterministic, automatic verification layer.
\item \textbf{Domain adaptation compounds.} LegalBERT improves on BERT \cite{chalkidis2020}; CaseLawBERT clarifies the conditions \cite{zheng2021}; InLegalBERT improves on both for Indian tasks \cite{paul2023}.
\item \textbf{Statutory cross-references defeat flat semantic retrieval.} Articles that merely reference other articles are effectively invisible to embedding similarity — confirmed independently by error analysis \cite{rustamov2025} and by graph-traversal gains \cite{bindra2026}.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Research Gaps}

From the above we identify five gaps, each a documented absence rather than a speculation, and each mapped in Table~\ref{tab:gaps} to a corresponding contribution.

\textbf{G1 --- No deterministic citation verification.} Across all six applied systems, hallucination control is statistical (adversarial evaluators, confidence weighting) or partly manual. No system enforces by construction that every generated citation resolves to a retrieved source, that quoted spans string-match their sources, and that cited provisions carry current repeal status.

\textbf{G2 --- No system spans statutes \emph{and} case law with recodification mapping.} BNS Mitra and Nyaya-Sathi cover only the BNS; Constitutional Compass only the Constitution; Dual-Layer covers judgments and the CrPC. The recodification is acknowledged as a motivating problem \cite{dixit2025} but no surveyed system implements a bidirectional old--new section mapping with automatic transition annotation.

\textbf{G3 --- No calibrated abstention in legal RAG.} Confidence mechanisms are demonstrated in open-domain QA \cite{sun2025}, and recall ceilings of 0.49--0.60 \cite{rustamov2025} show why they are needed in law, yet none of the legal systems can report insufficient authority. Given the finding that models cannot self-diagnose legal error \cite{dahl2024}, this gate must be external.

\textbf{G4 --- Incomparable, non-standard evaluation.} The applied systems report bespoke metrics on private query sets. None uses AILA or IL-TUR community relevance judgments, and none applies RAGAS-style faithfulness decomposition, making cross-system comparison unreliable.

\textbf{G5 --- Statutory cross-reference structure unexploited.} Rustamov \textit{et al.} diagnose the problem and defer graphs to future work; Bindra \textit{et al.} construct graphs over \emph{judgment} citations but not statutory cross-references.

\section{Proposed System}
\label{sec:proposed}

\subsection{Corpus Construction}

\subsubsection{Statutes} Central Acts are parsed from the India Code repository into a hierarchy of Act $\rightarrow$ Chapter $\rightarrow$ Section $\rightarrow$ Sub-section. Each node carries metadata: act name, act number and year, section number, marginal note, amendment history, repeal status, and — for criminal law — the old$\leftrightarrow$new section mapping. India Code is the only source carrying the BNS/BNSS/BSA and the superseded IPC/CrPC/IEA side by side, which makes it indispensable to C2.

\subsubsection{Judgments} Supreme Court judgments are drawn from ILDC \cite{malik2021}, with case name, neutral and reporter citations, bench, date, statutes referred, and body text extracted. Segmentation by rhetorical role (facts, issues, reasoning, ruling) is applied heuristically.

\subsubsection{Normalisation} Citation strings are canonicalised to a single internal form — ``Sec.\ 420 IPC'', ``s.420 of the Indian Penal Code'', and ``section 420, IPC'' all resolve to \texttt{IPC\S420}. This normalisation is a precondition for the verifier: a citation cannot be checked against a corpus unless both are expressed in a common referential vocabulary.

\subsubsection{Chunking} Chunking is structure-aware rather than fixed-size. Statutes are chunked one section per chunk — the section being the natural unit of citation — with the parent Act and Chapter title attached for context, and very long sections split at sub-section boundaries with overlap. Judgments are chunked at paragraph level with parent-document retrieval: the paragraph is the retrieval unit, but the surrounding context is supplied to the generator. This choice is a direct correction of the recursive character splitting used in \cite{patil2025}, and follows the length-distribution-informed methodology of \cite{rustamov2025}.

\subsection{Retrieval Architecture}

At query time the system executes seven stages.

\textbf{Stage 1 --- Query understanding.} The query is classified as statute lookup, case search, or open legal question; act and section entities are extracted; and the query is optionally rewritten into formal legal register, following \cite{patil2025}.

\textbf{Stage 2 --- Hybrid retrieval.} BM25 and dense retrieval run in parallel and are merged by Reciprocal Rank Fusion. Where Stage 1 detects a specific act or section, metadata filtering is applied before retrieval. Two embedding models are compared — InLegalBERT \cite{paul2023} as the domain-adapted arm and a strong general model as the control — constituting a reportable experiment in its own right.

\textbf{Stage 3 --- Cross-encoder reranking.} The fused candidate list is reranked from top-20 to top-5. Following the finding in \cite{rustamov2025} that reranking can improve NDCG while degrading MRR, we report both.

\textbf{Stage 4 --- Confidence gate (C3).} If the post-rerank confidence score falls below a threshold $\tau$ tuned on a development split, the system abstains with an explicit insufficient-authority response rather than generating.

\textbf{Stage 5 --- Generation.} A mid-size instruction-tuned model generates the answer under a prompt forbidding outside knowledge, requiring inline citation markers, and requiring verbatim quotation in quotation marks.

\textbf{Stage 6 --- Citation verification (C1).} Described in Section~\ref{sec:verifier}.

\textbf{Stage 7 --- Response assembly.} The verified answer is returned with expandable source cards showing the supporting span highlighted in the original section text, a recodification banner where applicable, and a prominent disclaimer that the system is a research and citation tool rather than a substitute for legal advice.

\subsection{The Deterministic Citation Verifier}
\label{sec:verifier}

The verifier is the paper's central contribution. It is deliberately model-free: it performs no inference, makes no probabilistic judgment, and therefore cannot itself hallucinate. Let $A$ be the generated answer, $R = \{r_1,\dots,r_k\}$ the set of retrieved chunks supplied to the generator, $M$ the metadata database, and $\theta = 0.95$ the quotation similarity threshold.

\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\STATE \textbf{Input:} answer $A$, retrieved set $R$, metadata DB $M$
\STATE \textbf{Output:} verified answer $A'$, verification report $V$
\STATE $A' \leftarrow A$; $V \leftarrow \emptyset$
\FOR{each citation marker $[n]$ in $A$}
    \IF{$n$ does not index a chunk in $R$}
        \STATE strip the associated claim from $A'$; log to $V$
    \ENDIF
\ENDFOR
\FOR{each legal reference $c$ parsed from $A$}
    \STATE $c \leftarrow \mathrm{normalise}(c)$
    \IF{$c \notin M$}
        \STATE strip claim; log unresolved reference to $V$
    \ELSE
        \IF{$\mathrm{repealed}(c) \vee \mathrm{amended}(c)$}
            \STATE annotate $A'$ with status and successor provision
        \ENDIF
    \ENDIF
\ENDFOR
\FOR{each quoted span $q$ in $A$}
    \STATE $s \leftarrow \max_{r \in R} \mathrm{sim}(q, r)$
    \IF{$s < \theta$}
        \STATE strip or unquote $q$; log to $V$
    \ENDIF
\ENDFOR
\RETURN $A'$, $V$
\end{algorithmic}

Three properties follow directly. First, citation precision on $A'$ is unity by construction, since any citation failing to resolve is removed before the answer is emitted. Second, the verifier is cheap: it performs string matching and database lookups, adding negligible latency compared to the 12.8\,s overhead reported for graph traversal and adversarial generation in \cite{bindra2026}. Third, it is auditable: the report $V$ records every intervention, so the system's failures are inspectable rather than opaque.

The cost is coverage. Stripping unverifiable claims necessarily reduces answer completeness, and this trade-off must be measured rather than assumed — hence our reporting of citation \emph{recall} alongside precision, and of coverage alongside abstention accuracy.

\subsection{Cross-Reference Resolution (C5)}

Following the diagnosis in \cite{rustamov2025}, intra-statute references (``punishment as provided in section X'') are resolved at indexing time and stored as metadata edges, so that a section which is not semantically self-contained remains reachable. As an extension, a combined statute--judgment citation graph supports ``cases interpreting this section'' expansion and an authority prior in reranking, following \cite{bindra2026}.

\subsection{Metric Families}

\textbf{Retrieval.} Recall@$\{5,10\}$, MRR, and nDCG@10, computed against AILA official relevance judgments and the IL-TUR statute-identification subset. Reporting MRR alongside nDCG is a deliberate response to the reranking anomaly documented in \cite{rustamov2025}.

\textbf{Citation.} Citation precision (the fraction of cited sources that support the adjacent claim) and citation recall (the fraction of gold citations recovered). Precision is expected to be unity post-verifier; recall measures the coverage cost of that guarantee.

\textbf{Faithfulness.} RAGAS \cite{es2023} faithfulness, answer relevance, and context precision, used diagnostically.

\textbf{Hallucination.} The proportion of answers containing any non-existent or unsupported legal reference, reported \emph{both} pre- and post-verifier. This comparison is the headline experiment.

\textbf{Abstention.} Accuracy on a set of deliberately unanswerable queries — for example, questions about state legislation outside the indexed corpus — together with the coverage--precision trade-off curve as $\tau$ varies.

\textbf{Adversarial robustness.} False-premise probes of the form ``Under Section 999 IPC\ldots'', following the contra-factual setup of \cite{dahl2024}, testing whether the system corrects or elaborates upon a false premise.

\subsection{Ablation Design}

The ablation proceeds cumulatively: BM25-only $\rightarrow$ $+$dense $\rightarrow$ $+$RRF fusion $\rightarrow$ $+$cross-encoder rerank $\rightarrow$ $+$confidence gate $\rightarrow$ $+$citation verifier, with an ungrounded LLM as the zero baseline. The ungrounded configuration is expected to reproduce, in the Indian context, the hallucination phenomenon documented for United States case law in \cite{dahl2024} and observed at 1.8\% citation accuracy in \cite{bindra2026}. The final increment isolates the contribution of C1.

\section{Implementation Status and Preliminary Results}
\label{sec:prelim}

Implementation is partial. This section reports what has been built and what
has been measured, and is explicit about what those measurements do and do not
establish.

\subsection{Scope of the Present Measurements}

Components implemented to date are the citation normaliser, the metadata store
with the recodification mapping, the deterministic verifier (all four checks),
the two repair rules, the advisory support screen with minimum-evidence
abstention, the cross-reference resolver and statutory citation graph, BM25 and
dense retrieval, RRF fusion, cross-encoder reranking, the confidence gate, and
a generator interface wired into a complete pipeline (Stages 1--7). An
end-to-end query path now exists in code and is exercised in full by
Section~\ref{ssec:pipeline}'s test suite, including, since the previous
revision, a real run against a live, independently invoked generator
(Section~\ref{ssec:live-generation}) rather than only the scripted stand-in
Section~\ref{ssec:pipeline}'s own regression tests still use for the reason
stated there (a paid API call cannot back a reproducible-without-credential
paper number). Every retrieval-quality number is measured on the real
statutory corpus of Section~\ref{ssec:realcorpus} rather than on hand-authored
probes. What has NOT been implemented is the judgment half of contribution
C2: Section~\ref{sec:proposed} describes paragraph-level chunking with
parent-document retrieval for Supreme Court judgments drawn from ILDC
\cite{malik2021}, and no judgment has been ingested against that design --
every retrieval and generation number in this paper is over the statutory
corpus alone. The RAPTOR-style long-document design this motivates
(Section~\ref{sec:related}, Thread B) is accordingly justified here only by
citation to other papers' measurements, not by this project's own.

All results below are computed on a \emph{development corpus} of 35
hand-entered provisions spanning ten central enactments and the Constitution.
This corpus exists to exercise the pipeline before full India Code ingestion.
Its section texts are paraphrased summaries rather than statutory language,
except for one provision entered verbatim so that the quotation check has a
genuine source to match against. Two consequences follow and are enforced in
code: the quotation check is disabled for paraphrased chunks rather than
silently passing them, and no measurement here should be read as a claim about
legal accuracy. What these numbers measure is \emph{pipeline behaviour}: whether
each component does what it is specified to do.

\subsection{The Real Corpus: De-Saturating Retrieval}
\label{ssec:realcorpus}

Every measurement in the remainder of this section, and in the paper before
this revision, was taken on the 35-provision development corpus. This
subsection reports what changed when it was not.

\textbf{Sourcing.} India Code (indiacode.nic.in), this project's originally
intended and preferred source, returned 403/404 to every content path probed
from the development environment -- two independently sourced handle pages,
two PDF bitstreams, and an alternate render endpoint, across two different
Acts -- while the bare domain returned 200, consistent with automated-client
blocking rather than a wrong URL or a transient fault. Indian Kanoon
(indiankanoon.org) was used instead: a long-established, publicly reachable
Indian legal database whose robots.txt permits general crawling and which
already underlies several corpora this paper cites (ILDC, IL-TUR). Its markup
follows the Akoma Ntoso legislative-XML convention. A separately located
commercially-published commentary PDF was examined and REJECTED as a source:
it interleaves case-law annotation and footnotes into the statutory text and
is almost certainly under separate copyright from the bare Act, which would
risk exactly the kind of misattributed content this project exists to detect.

\textbf{Scale.} 3{,}263 real, verbatim provisions across all ten target Acts
(IPC, BNS, CRPC, BNSS, IEA, BSA, NIA, ITA, ICA, and the Constitution), against
35 paraphrased ones. BNSS was initially believed to require a bespoke parser
for a harder two-column PDF bill format and was left out of the first
revision of this corpus on that assumption; the assumption was wrong; its
full text turned out to exist on Indian Kanoon in the same Akoma Ntoso
structure the other nine Acts use, and reusing the existing parser closed the
gap without new code.

\textbf{A third round of adversarial correction, found by using the corpus
rather than only auditing the parser.} Two prior rounds (Section~\ref{ssec:evasion})
found and fixed defects by asking what construct could defeat the citation
parser. This one was found a different way: while composing real generated
answers for the experiment in Section~\ref{ssec:generation}, a retrieved
chunk labelled BNS~\S317 (``Stolen property'') ran on into text that was
unmistakably \S318's cheating definition, not stolen-property law. The cause
was a FOURTH markup variant: Indian Kanoon renders BNS~\S318 as two
\texttt{<section class="akn-chapter">} tags (``Chapter Of cheating'', then
``Chapter 318. Cheating.'') instead of the usual section/heading pattern every
other provision uses. That tag class was never in the parser's collected set,
so \S318's heading was invisible and its body -- real sibling content --
was silently attributed to \S317, the last section the parser had actually
opened. This is the same CLASS of defect as the original BNS defect (a markup
variant the parser did not handle) but a more dangerous SHAPE: that one
produced zero sections and was caught by a section-count check; this one
produced a plausible, correctly-numbered corpus with one section's content
quietly merged onto its neighbour, invisible to any structural sweep that had
no reason to suspect \S317 was too long. \emph{This project had already
concluded, in the previous revision of this paper, that \S318 was a confirmed
source gap rather than a parser defect. That conclusion was wrong, and is
corrected here rather than silently amended.}

Investigating it surfaced a FIFTH, related variant: some inserted
sub-sections (IEA \S\S45A, 65A, 65B, 90A, 114A) are rendered as a bolded
run-in heading inside their parent section's own prose, with no section-level
tag boundary at all -- \S65's scraped body had silently absorbed five later
provisions this way, including \S65B, one of this project's own most-cited
example provisions (the BSA~\S63 mapping, the quotation-check discussion).
Fixed by extracting the exact heading strings from the raw markup before text
flattening loses the boundary, and splitting the already-parsed body at the
first exact occurrence of each one. Both fixes are covered by regression
tests naming the specific defect (\texttt{tests/test\_real\_corpus.py}), and
a corpus-wide scan confirms this markup pattern occurs in exactly six places,
all within IEA, five now split out and one (\S67A, whose source heading is
itself malformed -- an unclosed bracket) left as a small, stated residual gap
rather than chased further.

\textbf{A fourth round, found by auditing the corpus itself rather than the
parser or generated text.} Every defect above was found reactively --
adversarial probing of the parser, or an anomaly noticed while composing
generated text. This round instead swept all 3{,}263 provisions
systematically for structural anomalies (length outliers, self-duplication,
truncation), the highest-leverage unaudited surface this project's own
roadmap had already named. It found two further defects, both interactions
between the ``nested'' markup variant's two valid substructures rather than
an unhandled tag class. \textbf{S1: orphaned subsection.} Constitution
Article 334A (reservation of seats for women, added by the 2023 recodification
amendment) renders each subsection's numeral in its own tag but the
corresponding prose in a following SIBLING span, structurally invisible to a
walk that only descends into the numeral tag's own subtree; the scraped body
was the four bare numerals concatenated, ``1 2 3 4'', losing roughly 1{,}600
characters of substantive text. The same signature recurs as a
list-introducing clause that ends at the comma rather than continuing into
its own enumerated items -- BNS~\S326 (``Mischief by injury...''), whose body
resolved to ``Whoever commits mischief by,'' with clauses (a)/(b)/(c) one
sibling over. \textbf{S2: nested subsections double-counted.} For sections
where subsections ARE properly nested inside their parent tag (the ordinary,
usually-correct case), the section-level grab already contains every
subsection's text, and the parser's per-subsection pass then re-appends each
one again. Most such duplicates are an exact whole-body repeat that this
project's existing token-level deduplication pass already collapsed; IEA~\S146
exposed the general case, where a unique introductory clause precedes the
duplicated part, making the two halves unequal in length and invisible to an
exact-midpoint check. Generalised to search for a duplicated tail of any
length, not only an exact half, fixing IEA~\S146 -- and, in combination with
S1's fix, exposing a THIRD, sharper case: BNSS~\S266, where subsection (3)'s
own nested body was itself orphaned in S1's sense, so rescuing it from its
sibling without also recognising that the section-level grab already
contained subsections (1) and (2) would have reintroduced the very
duplication being removed. Resolved by deduplicating the parser's own list of
body parts before joining them, where the redundancy is unambiguous, rather
than pattern-matching the flattened string after the fact. Twelve provisions
across BNS, BNSS, BSA, IEA, and the Constitution are confirmed fixed by S1
and S2 together. The same audit flagged two further provisions
(IPC~\S99, ICA~\S35) on a cruder ``repeated opening text''
heuristic that turned out to be real statutory drafting -- IPC~\S99 restates
its own opening formula verbatim for two different actors before a
third, differently-worded clause -- reported here as a genuine boundary of
the heuristic rather than silently dropped, in the same spirit as this
project's other threshold-calibration findings. A third provision the same
heuristic flagged, IEA~\S119, was \emph{not} a false positive; it is fixed
in the next round below.

\textbf{A fifth round, re-running the same systematic sweep rather than
extending it with a new heuristic.} Re-auditing after S1/S2 found four
further defects, none caught by the length-outlier or duplication checks
that found S1/S2, because each corrupts a provision's text in place rather
than truncating or duplicating it. \textbf{S3: glued adjacent clauses.}
BeautifulSoup's default \texttt{get\_text()} inserts no whitespace between
sibling tags, and the source markup for enumerated list clauses routinely
carries none of its own either --
\texttt{<span>...or</span><span>is of such a nature...</span>} -- so
ICA~\S23 read ``\emph{oris of such a nature}'' and ``\emph{orinvolves}''
verbatim, corrupting exactly the text V4 exists to verify. Fixed by passing
an explicit space separator to \texttt{get\_text()}. \textbf{S4:
heading-embedded amendment history.} Marginal notes are extracted from the
same heading tag as the section number, but unlike the body-text path this
extraction never stripped Indian Kanoon's own editorial remarks, so
CRPC~\S166A's marginal note read ``\emph{Letter of request... [Inserted by
Act 10 of 1990, ...]}''. CRPC~\S330 is the more severe sibling case: with no
\texttt{data-status="editorial"} span to mark it at all, the ENTIRE
pre-amendment text of the section -- not a note, the whole 1973 wording --
was dumped unmarked into the heading. \textbf{S5: whole-section loss when
the rule lives in the heading.} Some sections carry their entire operative
text inside the heading tag itself, with no separate content container at
all; the body-extraction path correctly excludes heading text for every
ordinary section, but here that left the body empty, and the existing
\texttt{if body} check silently dropped the section entirely -- not a wrong
citation, an invisible one, confirmed at ICA~\S184 (in-force law) and, in a
milder disguise where a separate ``Illustrations'' block kept the section's
body non-empty while still missing its actual rule, ICA~\S68. Five
genuinely repealed IPC sections (\S\S56, 58, 161, 165, 492) shared the same
silent-loss shape; each is now rescued as an honest repeal record rather
than vanishing without trace. \textbf{S6: superseded text leaking into
current body.} Indian Kanoon renders a sub-section's pre-amendment wording
as a plain table or inline bracketed prose sitting inside the CURRENT
section, unmarked as historical -- IEA~\S119's body silently included both
its 2013-amended text and, concatenated after it, the entire pre-2013
wording. A fabricated quotation drawn from the superseded wording would
have validated against a citation to the current section, the same failure
V4 exists to prevent. Fixed for the unambiguous ``prior to substitution
... read as under'' marker only; a related, fuzzier construct -- tables of
state- and union-territory-specific amendments sharing the same markup with
no single reliable marker separating them from genuinely current schedule
content -- is confirmed present but deliberately left unfixed rather than
risk stripping real law with an under-tested heuristic. Two smaller,
non-corrupting fixes accompanied this round: a soft hyphen
(U+00AD, an invisible print-only hyphenation hint) found splitting 137 words
across the corpus, e.g.\ IPC~\S31's ``\emph{docu\-ment}'', invisible to a
human reader but a real character defeating exact-token quotation matching;
and a leading-bracket strip that had been removing only the opening ``['' of
a wrapped amendment marker, leaving an orphaned closing ``]'' on five of the
S5-rescued repeal notices. All eleven provisions changed by this round are
covered by named regression tests in \texttt{tests/test\_real\_corpus.py}
rather than asserted only in this text.

\textbf{Retrieval away from saturation.} BM25 Recall@1 on the ten-Act real
corpus is 0.375, against 1.000 on the toy corpus. This falls BELOW the
0.49--0.60 range this paper had cited from Rustamov \textit{et al.} for
structurally comparable statutory corpora as an expectation rather than a
measurement -- this corpus is harder than that reference range, not easier.
Recall was 0.500, inside the range, on the nine-Act corpus before BNSS was
added; including BNSS's 531 real procedural provisions, which lexically
compete with CRPC's on exactly the queries about arrest and FIR procedure
that used to resolve correctly, is what moved it below. V4 quotation checking
is now exercisable against 3{,}263 verbatim provisions rather than the single
hand-entered Article 21; the cross-reference graph finds 2{,}086 real edges
and 249 real provisions with dependency score above 0.5, against 7 synthetic
edges and 5 authored ones -- direct resolution of the equivocal-graph concern
raised in the original corpus caveats, that synthetic non-self-contained
provisions retain descriptive marginal notes real ones would not.

\textbf{Recodification mapping.} A matched series of three UP Police
``Corresponding Section Table'' documents -- covering BNS$\leftrightarrow$IPC,
BNSS$\leftrightarrow$CRPC, and BSA$\leftrightarrow$IEA, the three pairs this
project tracks -- was located, parsed, and cross-checked against all eleven
mapping entries this project already tracked. All eleven confirm exactly,
including CRPC~\S41$\rightarrow$BNSS~\S35 and IEA~\S65B$\rightarrow$BSA~\S63,
both unverified in the previous revision for lack of a located source. One
required a genuine refinement to the check rather than a discrepancy in the
mapping: the official table specifies IPC~\S379~$\rightarrow$~BNS~\S303(2), a
sub-section-precise entry, while this corpus indexes one chunk per section
(Section~\ref{sec:proposed}) and so has no ``BNS\S303(2)'' to point a mapping
at -- BNS~\S303's single chunk already contains both the definition IPC~\S378
and the punishment IPC~\S379 separately supplied. Verification is now
section-granularity-aware: a source entry confirms our own mapping when it
matches exactly, or when it is a sub-section refinement of a mapping we
record at whole-section granularity, never the reverse.

\subsection{Dense Retrieval: A Domain-Adaptation Negative Result}
\label{ssec:dense}

Two embedding arms were compared on the real corpus, as promised in
Section~\ref{sec:related}: InLegalBERT \cite{paul2023}, further pre-trained on
Indian legal text, against \texttt{all-MiniLM-L6-v2}, a general-purpose
sentence encoder with no legal-domain adaptation. Both are mean-pooled,
L2-normalised, and compared by exact cosine similarity.

The result is the opposite of what domain adaptation is usually assumed to
buy, and it is worth stating precisely rather than softening: InLegalBERT
underperforms not only the general-purpose encoder but plain BM25.

The likely mechanism is a known one, and it is a methodological lesson rather
than a defect in the corpus or the harness. InLegalBERT is a masked-language-
modelling checkpoint further pre-trained on legal text; it was never
fine-tuned with a retrieval or sentence-similarity objective. Raw mean-pooled
embeddings from an MLM-only encoder are well documented to be poorly suited to
cosine-similarity ranking -- this is precisely the motivation for
contrastive/Siamese fine-tuning approaches (Sentence-BERT and its
successors), which \texttt{all-MiniLM-L6-v2} received and InLegalBERT did not.
Domain-adapted pre-training and retrieval-tuned pre-training are different
axes, and this ablation shows they do not substitute for one another: legal
vocabulary familiarity does not compensate for the absence of a
similarity-shaped training signal. A domain-adapted SENTENCE encoder
(InLegalBERT fine-tuned with a retrieval objective, rather than InLegalBERT's
base MLM checkpoint used directly) is the comparison this result actually
motivates, and it is not one this project has run.

Fusion outperforms both single-arm configurations on every reported metric,
confirming the expectation this paper previously stated on the strength of
\cite{bolla2026,rustamov2025,sun2025} alone (Section~\ref{sec:outcomes}, prior
revision). Reranking's effect on MRR and nDCG is NOT the same in magnitude:
nDCG improves substantially (+0.038) while MRR moves only marginally
(+0.005) and Recall@1 does not move at all (0.562 before and after). This
paper committed in advance to reporting both metrics because Rustamov
\textit{et al.} found them capable of diverging in direction; what is
observed here is milder than that -- both move the same way, but by very
different amounts, which is closer to the divergence this paper's methodology
was designed to catch than the earlier nine-Act measurement showed, where
both metrics moved together by comparable amounts. Sixteen gold queries is
not enough to conclude the full divergence Rustamov \textit{et al.} report
would appear on a larger query set, only that reranking's benefit here is
concentrated in ranking QUALITY among already-retrieved results (nDCG) rather
than in promoting the single best result to rank 1 (MRR, Recall@1).

\subsection{End-to-End Pipeline Wiring}
\label{ssec:pipeline}

Every stage the architecture promises now exists in code and is connected:
confidence gate, fused and reranked retrieval, a generator interface, and the
citation verifier, in \texttt{pipeline.py}. This closes the gap stated in
every prior revision of this section, that no end-to-end query path existed.

It closes it only partially, and the remaining gap is stated precisely rather
than elided. No LLM API credential (Anthropic or otherwise) is available in
the development environment. \texttt{generation/generator.py} defines a
narrow \texttt{LLMClient} interface, a prompt contract requiring the same
things the verifier checks (inline \texttt{[n]} markers, verbatim quotation,
no outside knowledge), and a real client (\texttt{AnthropicClient}) that
raises immediately, rather than silently fabricating a response, when
unconfigured. Every pipeline-level test therefore substitutes a scripted FAKE
client that returns a pre-written string and records what it was called with
-- not a model, a fixed function of its script. This proves the WIRING: that
the gate abstains before generation runs on an unanswerable query, that
retrieved chunks reach the prompt with correctly numbered markers, and,
load-bearingly, that a fabricated citation the (scripted) generator emits is
still stripped by the verifier rather than trusted because it came from
`the generator'. It does not and cannot demonstrate what an actual language
model would produce, and no number in this paper should be read as claiming
that it does. Running this pipeline against a real model, and the pre/post-
verifier hallucination comparison that would make possible, is this project's
single largest remaining piece of work (Tier 2, item 10) and is unchanged by
everything else reported in this revision.

\subsection{Citation Normalisation}

The normaliser was evaluated on 48 held-out surface forms covering abbreviated
and expanded act names, pre- and post-posed section words, sub-section and
letter suffixes, the \texttt{u/s} shorthand, and citations embedded in running
prose; plus 8 negative strings containing bare numerals and legal vocabulary but
no citation. It resolves 48/48 positives (100\%) with 0/8 false positives.

Two defects were found and fixed during this evaluation, both instances of the
same root cause: a greedy optional letter-suffix in the section-number pattern.
It captured the ``o'' of ``of'' in ``Article 14 of the Constitution'' (yielding
\texttt{CONST\S14O}) and the ``C'' of ``Cr.P.C.''. A third defect arose from a
trailing word-boundary assertion, which fails after an abbreviation ending in a
period. These are reported because they illustrate a general point: citation
normalisation for legal text is not a solved string-processing problem, and the
verifier's guarantee is only as strong as the normaliser beneath it.

\subsection{Quotation Verification: A Negative Result}

Quotation checking was initially implemented with character-level partial
fuzzy matching, the conventional choice. It failed on a probe that substitutes
a single word in an eighteen-word quotation from Article 21, replacing
``procedure established by law'' with ``procedure established by statute''. The
substitution inverts the provision's meaning, yet scores 98.1\% by character
partial ratio and 95.9\% by full character ratio, passing every threshold in
$[0.80, 0.98]$.

The diagnosis is that character-level similarity is structurally unsuited to
verbatim quotation checking: a long correct prefix dominates the score and a
short meaning-bearing substitution is lost in the average. We therefore score at
token granularity, as the ratio of the longest contiguous run of quote tokens
found in the source to the total quote token count. A single substitution in an
$n$-token quote then caps the score below $(n-1)/n$, which the threshold
rejects. Table~\ref{tab:theta} reports the resulting threshold sweep.

\subsection{Support Screening and Its Failure Boundary}
\label{ssec:support}

The limitation conceded in Section~IX --- that the verifier establishes that a
citation \emph{resolves}, not that it is \emph{apposite} --- is not hypothetical.
The sentence ``Murder is punishable under Section~138 of the NI~Act~[1]'' passes
V1 through V4 with zero findings: the marker resolves, the provision exists and
is in force, and no quotation is claimed. Only its substance is wrong, and it is
wrong in the most dangerous available way, being maximally well-formed.

Full entailment checking would require semantic judgement and so reintroduce a
model into the verification layer, forfeiting the property that makes the layer
trustworthy. We therefore implement a strictly weaker but deterministic test: a
necessary-condition screen. If a provision genuinely supports a claim, the
claim's substantive vocabulary should overlap the provision's subject matter.
The converse does not hold, so the screen is sound in one direction only. Terms
are weighted by inverse document frequency computed over the corpus itself, so
that structural drafting vocabulary contributes nothing and discriminative terms
dominate; citation spans are excised before scoring so that ``section'' and the
section number cannot inflate the result.

Table~\ref{tab:support} reports the central finding, which is a negative one.
Where the incorrect citation is drawn from an unrelated area of law, correct and
incorrect attributions are linearly separable with a comfortable margin. Where
the incorrect citation is \emph{topically adjacent} --- a definition claim cited
to the corresponding punishment section, a murder claim cited to the attempt
provision --- the distributions overlap, and no threshold achieves both
precision and recall. The best attainable F1 on the adjacent set is 0.83, at a
flagging precision of 0.71.

This is a principled boundary rather than an implementation defect. Adjacent
provisions share subject-matter vocabulary by construction, since that is what
makes them adjacent; the distinction between them is carried by grammatical role
(what the provision \emph{does} with the subject matter) rather than by lexical
content, and a bag-of-terms method cannot represent it.

The deployment consequence is that V5 must not strip. A threshold aggressive
enough to catch adjacent misattribution necessarily deletes correct claims,
which would trade the very guarantee the verifier exists to provide. We
therefore run V5 as an advisory annotation at $\tau_{s} = 0.20$. Flagging
precision remains 1.000 across the band $\tau_{s} \in [0.025, 0.225]$, and recall
plateaus at its maximum of 0.818 from $\tau_{s} = 0.150$ upward; we take
$\tau_{s} = 0.20$ as the point that attains that maximum while retaining margin
against the lowest-scoring correct claim in the adjacent set (0.24), beyond
which precision breaks. Evaluated end to end
through the verifier on 20 marker-attributed claims, this configuration flags 9
of 11 incorrect attributions (recall 0.818) and flags \emph{no} correct claim
(precision 1.000, 0 false positives). Two adjacent misattributions escape, and
we report them as escaping: the screen narrows the blind spot substantially but
does not close it, and claims about apposite citation remain outside what this
architecture guarantees.

A defect surfaced during integration and is worth recording. V5 initially
collected candidate provisions only from explicit textual citations, so a claim
attributed solely by a bare marker --- the common case in generated text --- was
never screened at all. The regression harness reported zero flags across the
entire support probe set before the omission was noticed. Candidate provisions
are now collected from both textual citations and marker-to-chunk resolution.

are reported because they illustrate that an evaluation set constructed
alongside the component it tests will systematically miss the cases its author
did not anticipate.

\subsection{Cross-Reference Resolution}
\label{ssec:xref}

The cross-reference resolver extracts 7 edges over the development corpus, all
intra-act references resolved against their enclosing act. A dependency
heuristic cleanly separates the five deliberately non-self-contained provisions
(scores $0.86$--$1.00$) from the remaining thirty (all $0.00$), confirming that
provisions whose operative content lies elsewhere are identifiable at index
time.

Retrieval impact is more equivocal, and we report it as such. Over five queries
whose gold answer is a non-self-contained provision, inbound graph expansion
improves Recall@1 from $0.600$ to $0.800$ ($+0.200$) at a candidate-set cost of
$1.0 \rightarrow 1.2$ documents, and yields no gain at $K \geq 2$. Recall on
the sixteen ordinary queries is unchanged, so expansion does not degrade the
common case.

We do not present this as a strong result. On a 35-provision corpus, $K=10$
returns 29\% of the entire collection, so any measurement above $K=1$ is
saturated and uninformative. Moreover our synthetic non-self-contained
provisions retain descriptive marginal notes (``Attempting to commit
offences''), which leak enough lexical signal for BM25 to find them --- whereas
the real instances Rustamov \textit{et al.} describe are unreachable precisely
because they carry no such signal. The mechanism is demonstrated to work; its
magnitude on a realistic corpus is untested.

\subsection{Parser Evasion: Twelve Defects in the Guarantee}
\label{ssec:evasion}

The checks of Section~IV can only fire on citations the parser extracts. A
citation that is never extracted is never checked, and no error is raised ---
the guarantee is void while the system reports success. This makes the parser,
not the checks, the weakest point in the architecture, and we audited it
adversarially. Three defects were found, all of which had been present
throughout the measurements reported above. A second audit, conducted after
the regression harness of Section~\ref{ssec:harness} was in place, found six
more; both audits are reported here because the second is evidence about the
first, namely that one adversarial pass over a parser is not sufficient.

\textbf{D1: sentence segmentation.} Splitting on sentence-final punctuation
shatters legal prose, which is dense with abbreviations ending in a period.
``The accused was charged under Sec.~420 IPC. He pleaded not guilty''
segmented into three fragments, severing the citation from its claim; the
orphaned fragment ``420 IPC.'' no longer parses as a citation, so V2 silently
never ran on it. Abbreviation periods are now masked before segmentation and
restored afterwards, against a list of the forms common in Indian legal
drafting.

\textbf{D2: enumerated references.} References of the form ``sections 302 and
304 of the IPC'' extracted \emph{nothing at all} --- not partially, but zero
citations --- because the trailing act name is separated from the first numeral
by the remainder of the list, defeating every pattern. A fabricated section
concealed inside an enumeration was therefore invisible to the existence check.
This is the most serious of the three: it is a hole in the guarantee rather
than a gap in coverage, and it is trivially exploitable by a generator that
happens to enumerate. Enumerations are now parsed and expanded, with numeric
ranges expanded only when both endpoints are plain integers and the span is
small; lettered ranges (``66A to 66F'') yield their endpoints alone, since
enumerating them would mean inventing provisions.

\textbf{D3: unterminated quotations.} The quotation pattern required a closing
mark, so a fabricated quotation missing one matched nothing and bypassed V4
entirely. The pattern now terminates at a closing mark or end of input.

The second audit proceeded by asking what a generator could emit that no
pattern covers, rather than by extending the probe set, and found six further
defects of the same kind. \textbf{D4: act-first enumerations.} ``BNS sections
318 and 7777'' and the bare ``BNS 318/7777'' are the mirror image of D2 --- the
act precedes the list, so the pattern matched the act and the \emph{first}
number only and silently discarded every later member. \textbf{D5: separator
coverage.} The enumeration separator set omitted the slash, the semicolon and
the ``r/w'' conjunction, all standard in Indian drafting, so a fabricated
section joined by any of them escaped extraction. \textbf{D6: inline markup.}
A section number wrapped in markdown emphasis (``Section **7777** BNS'') or a
backtick code span was invisible --- a construct language models emit
constantly. \textbf{D7: invisible characters.} A zero-width space between the
number and the act name defeated every pattern; unlike the others this is a
deliberate-evasion vector rather than a natural one. \textbf{D8: interposed
footnote markers.} ``Section 7777[2] BNS'' severed the number from its act.
\textbf{D9: parenthesised numbers.} ``Section (7777) BNS'' likewise matched
nothing.

D6 and D7 are repaired by a sanitising pass that blanks citation-irrelevant
characters before matching. The pass replaces them with spaces rather than
deleting them, because citation offsets are used by repair rule R2 to splice a
rewritten reference into the emitted sentence and by the support screen to
excise citation spans before scoring; a length-changing sanitiser would shift
those offsets and corrupt output. The remaining defects are repaired by
patterns. One further observation is worth recording: the first implementation
of the D4 fix reintroduced the \emph{same} greedy letter-suffix root cause as
the two normalisation defects of Section~VII-B, silently truncating
``103, 105 and 7777'' after its second member, and was caught only because the
probe asserted the extracted citation \emph{count} rather than the verdict.

A third audit differs from the first two in provenance rather than method: it
was not found by inspecting the parser at all, but by inspecting a genuinely
generated answer produced by an independently invoked model during this
project's demo build -- the first defects in this project found through real
usage rather than deliberate adversarial construction. \textbf{D10: repeated
section symbol in an act-first list.} A generator that writes ``the
Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita \S80, \S118'' -- repeating the section symbol per
item rather than dropping it after the first, arguably the clearer style for
a human reader -- defeated the act-first bare-enumeration pattern (the D4
fix) entirely: its list-continuation grammar expected a digit immediately
after each separator, so a literal ``\S'' where the second item began matched
zero repetitions, and the list silently collapsed to its first member.
\textbf{D11: hyphenated compound suffixes.} Indian statutory drafting
commonly attaches a sub-section letter with a hyphen (``Section 65-B'', the
Evidence Act's electronic-records provision used as a running example
throughout this paper), and this defect had two independent causes. First,
the single-citation bare-act pattern (matching forms like ``IEA \S65-B''
with no ``Section'' word) never supported a hyphenated suffix at all,
truncating the match to its bare digits. Second, even where a hyphenated
suffix \emph{was} captured correctly, the canonicalisation step kept the
hyphen instead of stripping it, producing a key no entry in the corpus uses
-- every compound-suffix key in the real corpus joins the letter directly,
with no separator. D11 is qualitatively worse than every defect before it:
D1--D10 are \emph{blind spots}, where a fabricated citation goes unchecked
because the parser never sees it. D11's first cause is a \emph{false
anchor} -- the truncated match, ``65'', is itself a real but wholly
unrelated provision in the full corpus (proof of cession of territory, not
electronic-records admissibility), so the wrong citation did not go
unchecked, it \emph{validated}, silently, against the wrong law. This is
only observable against the real 3{,}263-provision corpus of
Section~\ref{ssec:realcorpus}; the 35-provision development corpus used for
the probes below has no same-numeral collision pair to demonstrate it,
consistent with this paper's repeated point that a small, convenient corpus
can hide exactly the failures a system exists to catch. Both causes are
fixed by routing the single-citation number token through the same pattern
every other single-citation form already used, and by stripping rather than
preserving the hyphen during canonicalisation; a dedicated regression test
against the real corpus (Section~\ref{ssec:realcorpus}'s test suite)
reproduces the exact collision and confirms it no longer occurs.

A fourth round found one further defect, this time not from the demo build
but from the live-generation experiment of
Section~\ref{ssec:live-generation} itself -- the most paper-central source
yet, since this round's whole purpose was measurement, not adversarial
probing. \textbf{D12: act name, then a parenthesised section.} The
independently invoked model's real answer to a defamation query wrote ``the
Indian Penal Code (Section 500)''; the pattern matching an act name followed
by a section reference (P3) required a comma or ``of the'' between them, and
a literal ``('' satisfied neither, so the clause extracted no citation at
all on a real, existing provision (IPC~\S500). Fixed by making the
parenthesis optional on both sides of the section reference, which changes
nothing about the forms P3 already matched.

evaluation suite after these repairs changed \emph{no previously reported

alongside the component it exercises inherits that component's blind spots, and
a stable score across a correctness-changing edit is evidence of an
insufficiently adversarial evaluation rather than of robustness.

\subsection{Minimum-Evidence Abstention in the Support Screen}
\label{ssec:minev}

The evasion controls also exposed an over-firing failure in V5. Claims phrased
procedurally rather than substantively --- ``Charges were framed under sections
318 and 103 of the BNS'' --- retain almost no topical vocabulary once
structural terms are removed, so the support score collapses to zero and a
valid claim is flagged. Both valid controls were flagged, despite V5 having
measured a flagging precision of 1.000 on the probes designed for it.

The screen now abstains rather than guessing when a claim carries fewer than
three substantive terms, and the structural vocabulary has been extended to
cover auxiliaries and claim-framing verbs (``provides'', ``applies'',
``charged''), which describe the act of asserting rather than the subject
matter asserted. After both changes V5 retains flagging precision 1.000 and
recall 0.818 on its designed probes while flagging neither held-out control.
That the guard was necessary at all is a caution about the earlier figure: a
precision of unity measured on probes authored for the component is weaker
evidence than the same figure measured on inputs it was not designed against.

\subsection{Support via Cross-Reference: A Predicted Fix That Had to Be Rebuilt}
\label{ssec:xrefsupport}

The support screen of Section~\ref{ssec:support} and the citation graph of
Section~\ref{ssec:xref} were built independently and did not consult each
other, which leaves a gap that is structural rather than incidental. A
provision whose text merely refers elsewhere --- ``cognizance of any offence
punishable under section~138'' --- carries almost no topical vocabulary of its
own. A claim about the \emph{substance} of the offence, correctly cited to that
provision, therefore scores near zero and is flagged as unsupported. The false
alarm is produced by the citation being correct in exactly the way the graph
module exists to handle.

The size of the defect was larger than expected. On a probe set of correct and
incorrect citations to the five non-self-contained provisions in the
development corpus, V5 alone flags \emph{every one} of the five correct
citations: flagging precision on this class is 0.500, against the 1.000
measured on the probes authored for the component. This is the sharpest
available illustration of the caution stated in Section~\ref{ssec:minev} --- a
precision of unity measured on probes written for a mechanism is weak evidence
about inputs it was not designed against.

The obvious repair --- suppress the flag when a one-hop outbound reference
supports the claim --- was specified in advance. We built the stress set before
implementing it, following the procedure used for the adjacent-provision set,
and the measurement rejected the specification. Suppression costs flagging
precision, because \emph{the edge that rescues a correct citation is the same
edge that rescues a topically-adjacent wrong one}. A claim about when police
may arrest, wrongly cited to the procedure-after-arrest section, is rescued
through that section's express reference to the arrest power; on the existing
hard set this converts a correctly flagged misattribution into a silent pass.
Two of six deliberately wrong citations in the new set are rescued the same
way. This is the grammatical-role boundary already established for V5, and a
graph walk does not cross it, because the graph encodes which provisions refer
to which and not what role the reference plays.

We therefore do not suppress. Support-via-reference is reported as a third
verdict alongside \emph{supported} and \emph{weakly supported}: the annotation
states that the cited provision's own text does not support the claim and names
the provision it refers to that does. Nothing is silently passed and nothing
correct is mislabelled as unsupported; all three verdicts are advisory and none
strips, so invariant~4 is preserved. Table~\ref{tab:xrefsupport} reports the
outcome. Three of the five false alarms are removed and flagging precision on
the affected sets is unchanged at 1.000; the recall cost is one probe, which
moves from the flag category into the weaker annotation rather than out of the
report altogether.

Two of the five correct citations are still not recovered. Their referenced
provisions are themselves paraphrased summaries in this corpus and share too
little vocabulary with the claim to clear the threshold even one hop away. We
report this as a bound on the mechanism rather than tuning the probes until it
disappears: the integration helps exactly where the referenced provision
carries substantive text, and a corpus of real statutory language --- where
referenced provisions are long and specific --- is the condition under which it
would help most. That remains untested.

\subsection{Retrieval Baseline and a Recodification Diagnostic}

BM25 over the development corpus attains Recall@1 $=$ 1.000 and MRR@10 $=$
1.000 on 16 gold questions. This result is reported for completeness and should
be read as a saturated benchmark rather than a finding: with 35 provisions whose
marginal notes are lexically distinctive, retrieval is trivial. It establishes
that the pipeline runs end to end, nothing more, and we expect these numbers to
fall sharply on the full corpus toward the 0.49--0.60 range reported for
comparable statutory collections \cite{rustamov2025}.

A more discriminating diagnostic concerns the recodification. Where a query's
answer exists in both a repealed provision and its successor, the two share a
marginal note that is near-identical by construction --- ``Punishment for
murder'' heads both IPC~\S302 and BNS~\S103. A purely lexical retriever
therefore has no signal separating current from superseded law. Over the nine
gold questions with such a pair, BM25 ranks the in-force provision above its
repealed predecessor in only 3 of 9 cases (33.3\%), worse than chance. In the
worst instance the in-force provision falls to rank 23 while its repealed twin
is retrieved first.

Applying the verifier's mapping layer to the same nine cases, every citation to
a superseded provision is detected and annotated with its successor: 9/9
(100\%). The user is therefore informed which provision currently governs
irrespective of retrieval order. This is the clearest empirical support we have
so far for treating recodification handling as a distinct contribution rather
than an incidental feature: the failure is real, it is invisible to the
retrieval metrics conventionally reported, and it is addressed at the
verification layer rather than the retrieval layer.

We record one caveat on this result. The IPC$\leftrightarrow$BNS
correspondences in the development corpus were entered by hand and are flagged
in the database as pending verification against the official India Code
correspondence tables; the verifier surfaces that provisional status in its
annotations. The 100\% figure measures that the mapping mechanism works, not
that the mappings are legally correct.

\subsection{Calibrated Abstention (C3)}
\label{ssec:confidence}

Verification is silent about a query whose answer is not in the corpus at all.
The retriever still returns its top-$k$, a generator still writes over them, and
every citation in the result may verify perfectly while the answer is worthless:
nothing about the emitted text is malformed, so no check fires. The confidence
gate addresses this by declining to answer.

It must be labelled correctly. The gate is a threshold on a retrieval score,
tuned on held-out data --- a \emph{statistical} decision, and therefore a weaker
kind of guarantee than the verification layer provides. It is implemented in the
retrieval package rather than the verification package for that reason, and it
can err in both directions: refusing an answerable query costs coverage, and
answering an unanswerable one costs exactly what it exists to prevent.

A raw BM25 score is not comparable across queries, because it scales with how
many substantive terms the query contains and how rare they are. We therefore
normalise by the inverse document frequency the query puts on the table,

\[
\mathrm{conf}(q) \;=\; \frac{\mathrm{bm25}_{\text{top-1}}(q)}
                            {\sum_{t \in q} \mathrm{idf}(t)},
\]

which asks what fraction of the evidence a query demanded was actually found. A
term absent from the corpus contributes maximum demand and no score, which is
what drives an out-of-corpus query low rather than high.

Sixteen deliberately unanswerable queries were written --- real questions of
Indian law about tax, intellectual property, family law, company law, labour law
and limitation, none of which the development corpus covers --- and made hard on
purpose, sharing vocabulary such as ``property'', ``bail'', ``penalty'' and
``records'' with provisions that are in it. Answerable and unanswerable queries
were each split in half; every threshold is tuned on the development half and
reported on the held-out half.

Table~\ref{tab:confidence} reports the outcome, and the ordering is the point.
\emph{Selecting the signal by development-set performance would have chosen raw
BM25 top-1}, which separates the two classes perfectly on dev and then makes two
errors on test. The normalised signal looks worse on dev --- its classes
overlap slightly there --- and makes none. We deploy it on the prior argument
about comparability stated above rather than on this comparison, which is the
correct order of reasoning: with eight queries per class per split, a difference
of two queries is an illustration and not a significant result. We report the
comparison because the temptation to pick the signal that wins on the
development split is exactly the methodological error this paper attributes to
the surveyed literature, and it is available here too.

At the selected threshold $\tau_c = 0.305$ --- the midpoint of the
development-optimal interval rather than its edge, since an edge threshold sits
on an observed score --- the gate answers all eight held-out answerable queries
and abstains on all eight held-out unanswerable ones. On the development half it
answers one unanswerable query, which we report rather than absorbing into a
retuned threshold.

Two bounds should be stated plainly. The corpus is 35 provisions and its
retrieval benchmark is saturated, so this measures that the calibration
procedure is sound, not that the gate performs at this level; and the
unanswerable queries were authored by us, so they test the gate against
out-of-corpus questions we thought to ask. The genuinely adversarial case --- a
question whose answer is \emph{partially} in the corpus, where the correct
behaviour is neither a confident answer nor a refusal --- is not represented
here at all, and is the obvious next probe set to build.

\subsubsection{Recalibration on the Real Corpus}
\label{sssec:confidence-real}

The first of those two bounds is no longer purely theoretical: an
ad-hoc check of $\tau_c = 0.305$ against the real, 3{,}263-provision corpus of
Section~\ref{ssec:realcorpus} answers ``How is agricultural income taxed?''
--- a query the toy-corpus gate correctly refuses --- because the larger
corpus's different score distribution is not the one the threshold was tuned
against. A threshold does not transfer across corpora of different scale by
assumption, and this is a concrete instance of that, not a hypothetical one.

We therefore recalibrate independently on the real corpus, holding the
methodology fixed: the same sixteen answerable queries (Section~\ref{sec:data}'s
gold set, whose keys exist in the real corpus as a superset of the
development one) and the same sixteen unanswerable queries, split 8/8 into
dev and test, the threshold swept and selected on dev only. The result is a
second, independently-tuned threshold, $\tau_r = 1.088$ --- not assumed equal
to $\tau_c$, and not equal to it, because the real corpus's much larger and
more lexically competitive vocabulary shifts the raw score distribution the
normalised signal is computed from. On the held-out test split, the
recalibrated gate answers 7 of 8 answerable queries and abstains on all 8
unanswerable ones (balanced accuracy 0.938, zero unanswerable queries
wrongly answered, one answerable query wrongly refused). The same
methodological finding recurs: the signal with the best \emph{development}
balanced accuracy (raw top-1) is again not the signal that generalises best
to test, reproducing on a second, unrelated corpus the exact caution this
project attributes to the surveyed literature. The recalibrated gate is
deployed on the Streamlit demo's live-generation tab (a presentation aid, not
part of this paper's evaluation) in place of the earlier design choice to
skip gating entirely rather than misapply an out-of-domain threshold; see
eval/run\_confidence\_real\_eval.py.

This does not close the second bound above --- the unanswerable queries
remain author-written, and the partially-answerable case remains unbuilt ---
but it does convert the first bound from an acknowledged theoretical gap into
a measured number, in both directions: the gate demonstrably fails when
misapplied across corpora, and demonstrably recovers when independently
recalibrated.

\subsubsection{An Out-of-Sample Check on Externally-Sourced Queries}

The ``author-written'' half of the second bound has a partial answer that
does not require a larger corpus: eight genuinely externally-sourced
unanswerable queries, the verbatim titles of real question-and-answer pages
on law4u.in, a public Indian legal Q\&A platform, kept alongside their source
URLs specifically so the claim that this project did not write them is
checkable rather than asserted. The deployed gate ($\tau_r=1.088$) is applied
exactly as calibrated --- no threshold here is fit to these eight queries,
which is the entire point of the check.

Six of eight correctly abstain; 2 of eight do not, giving a correct-abstention rate
of 0.750 against the perfect 1.000 measured on the author-written test
split. We report this as a real, informative degradation rather than
selecting a friendlier set: a suspiciously perfect score on a genuinely
external probe set would be less credible, not more. Both failures are
explicable rather than arbitrary. ``What is the limitation period for
filing a recovery suit in India?'' scores high because BNSS~\S514 genuinely
discusses limitation periods --- for criminal cognizance, not the civil
Limitation Act the question means --- the same class of topically-adjacent
collision the support screen's hard-set margin already documents, arising
here in retrieval rather than in citation attribution. ``How Do the
Provisions of Section~56(2) of the Income Tax Act Apply to Gifts?'' fails
differently: this corpus's own alias table resolves ``ITA'' to the
Information Technology Act, not the Income Tax Act, and generic procedural
vocabulary (``provisions'', ``apply'') combined with that act-name collision
scores just high enough to slip through --- an acronym collision the
generator side of this system has no visibility into, since the gate
operates on the query alone.

Bounds on this check should be stated as plainly as the result: eight
queries from one platform is not a large or maximally adversarial sample,
and a Q\&A site plausibly shapes its own question titles for search
visibility, which is a different kind of authorship bias than this
project's own, not an absence of one. It is, nonetheless, the first time
this gate has been measured against text this project did not write in any
form, and the result is neither the false reassurance a perfect score would
have given nor a silent failure --- it is a specific, explained boundary,
in keeping with how every other threshold in this paper is reported.

\subsection{The Pre/Post-Verifier Comparison, Run for the First Time}
\label{ssec:generation}

Section~\ref{ssec:pipeline} states plainly that the pipeline had never been
exercised with a real language model, for lack of an available API
credential. This subsection reports a partial resolution rather than leaving
that gap untouched, and states its limits at least as prominently as its
result.

\textbf{Method.} No credential became available. Instead, sixteen real,
unscripted answers were composed -- by Claude Opus 5, the model driving this
research session, invoked directly rather than through
\texttt{generation/generator.py}'s API interface -- to every query in the
gold set, under two conditions matching this paper's committed ablation
design: UNGROUNDED, answered from the model's own knowledge with no retrieved
context, and GROUNDED, answered strictly from the actual top-5 chunks the
real pipeline (BM25 + dense, RRF-fused, reranked) retrieves for that query
against the 3{,}263-provision corpus, under the exact prompt contract
\texttt{generation/generator.py} defines. Retrieval quality was not curated:
some queries retrieved the exact on-point provision at rank 1, several
retrieved only adjacent or procedural provisions, and one (FIR registration)
retrieved nothing relevant at all, and the recorded answers reflect that
variation honestly rather than reproducing the gold key regardless of what
was retrieved. Both conditions were then run through the unmodified
\texttt{CitationVerifier}, and citation counts were re-derived independently
of the verifier's own report, in the same style as the audit function in
Section~\ref{sec:prelim}'s repair ablation.

\textbf{This is not a blinded evaluation, and the numbers below should not be
read as one.} The generating model is the same session that built the
corpus, the verifier, and the gold answer key, with full visibility into all
three while composing these answers, and the query set itself skews toward
well-known, heavily-discussed landmark provisions (murder, Article 21, cheque
dishonour). A true replication needs an independently invoked model with no
such visibility. What this run does demonstrate is that the wiring behaves
correctly under genuine, unscripted natural-language generation rather than
only the scripted fake client of Section~\ref{ssec:pipeline}'s tests -- more
than that suite alone could show, and honestly less than an unbiased estimate
of real-world hallucination rates.

marker in either condition. This is a narrower and more honest finding than
``the verifier caught extensive hallucination'': it did not need to, here,
which is itself evidence for the stated limitation above, since a naive,
uninvolved model on an unfamiliar query distribution would be expected to
fabricate considerably more. The demonstrated value of the run lies elsewhere,
in three places the verifier's other mechanisms actually fired on real text
for the first time:

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Repair fired on genuine generated prose.} Every ungrounded
answer citing a since-repealed Act (9 of 16; the other 7 named already-current
Acts) was automatically repointed to its current-law successor with a
transparent parenthetical, by R2, unprompted -- the mechanism the paper
describes was exercised on text it was never shown in advance, not only on
probes authored alongside it.
\item \textbf{V3 correctly declined to strip an unmapped repealed citation,
surfacing a real gap of its own.} The ungrounded defamation answer cited
``Sections 499--502 of the IPC'' as a range; only \S499 has a recorded BNS
successor in this project's mapping table, so \SS500--502 were correctly
annotated as superseded with no mapping recorded rather than silently dropped
or falsely resolved -- an honest limitation of this project's mapping
coverage, exposed by real text rather than hidden by a probe that never
happened to construct this exact case.
\item \textbf{V4 caught a real quotation error.} The grounded answer to the
defamation query quoted a retrieved chunk at 91.7\% token-match, below the
0.95 threshold, over a small wording deviation introduced while composing the
answer -- not a planted fault, an actual mistake -- and the sentence was
correctly stripped, which is why that query's coverage falls to 0.50 while
every other grounded query holds at 1.00.
\end{itemize}

The headline pre/post-verifier hallucination comparison this paper's abstract
and introduction motivate -- via an independently invoked API call, on a
query distribution not curated by the generating model's own operator --
has a first real answer, reported next; it is a genuine methodological
advance over this subsection's round, not yet the full promised ablation,
and Section~\ref{sec:limitations} states exactly what still separates the two.

\subsection{The Same Comparison, Against an Independently Invoked Generator}
\label{ssec:live-generation}

This subsection's round used this project's own model to compose answers,
with full visibility into the corpus, the verifier, and the gold key while
writing them -- disclosed above as neither blinded nor representative.
Section~\ref{ssec:pipeline}'s wiring makes a different round possible: a real
API call to a model this project did not build, invoked through
\texttt{generation/generator.py}'s unmodified interface, seeing nothing but
the query and whatever the real pipeline actually retrieves.

\textbf{Method.} Every one of the sixteen gold queries (Section~\ref{sec:data})
and all sixteen deliberately unanswerable queries
(Section~\ref{ssec:confidence}) were run through the deployed system exactly
as a real user would experience it: the real-corpus confidence gate
($\tau_r=1.088$, Section~\ref{sssec:confidence-real}), then --- for whatever
passed the gate --- BM25+dense retrieval, RRF fusion, and cross-encoder
reranking against the real 3{,}263-provision corpus, then a live call to
\texttt{openai/gpt-oss-20b} via Groq, then the unmodified verifier. The query
set is still project-authored (Section~\ref{sec:data}'s gold set was written
before retrieval was tuned, specifically to avoid bias toward what the system
happens to retrieve, which bounds but does not remove that authorship), so
this is a genuine advance in \emph{generator independence}, not yet in
\emph{query-set independence}; the full promised ablation on an
externally-sourced adversarial set remains the larger undertaking
Section~\ref{sec:limitations} describes.

\textbf{The gate, applied end to end.} All sixteen unanswerable queries were
correctly refused; zero were wrongly answered. Applied to the full gold set
rather than only the held-out test split, the gate passed 10 of 16 queries to
generation and refused 6 --- a materially lower coverage than the 0.875
reported on the held-out split alone (Section~\ref{sssec:confidence-real}),
because the dev-split queries were never held out from the threshold that was
ultimately selected and several score close to $\tau_r$ on short, colloquially
phrased questions (e.g. ``What happens if a cheque bounces due to
insufficient funds?'', score 0.661) that carry few of the rare substantive
terms the IDF-normalised signal rewards. This is reported as a real coverage
cost, not smoothed into the test-split figure: a deployed system built on
this gate will refuse more genuinely answerable questions than the held-out
number alone suggests, concentrated in colloquial phrasing.

\textbf{Citation resolution.} Across the ten generated answers, pre-verifier
resolution accuracy is 1.000 (27/27) and post-verifier resolution accuracy is
1.000 (21/21) --- the core guarantee (V1/V2/V4) holds, and unlike this
subsection's earlier round, this model fabricated no nonexistent provision
and used no unresolved marker on its own, unprompted, with no visibility
into what would be checked.

\textbf{What actually moved coverage: quotation, not existence.} Thirteen
findings fired across the ten answers: three repealed citations (V3,
correctly annotated rather than stripped), two weak-support flags (V5,
advisory), and \emph{eight} unsupported-quotation findings (V4) --- against
one such finding in the sixteen answers of the earlier, self-composed round.
\texttt{openai/gpt-oss-20b} wraps text in quotation marks considerably more
often than it reproduces the source verbatim inside them, paraphrasing while
presenting the result as a direct quote; V4 caught every instance,
correctly stripping each one, which is why mean coverage across the ten
generated answers falls to 0.930 rather than holding at 1.000. This is the
strongest evidence yet that the mechanism does real work rather than passing
because it was rarely tested: a smaller, independently invoked, blind model
produces exactly the failure mode V4 exists to catch, at a rate an order of
magnitude higher than anything seen in hand-composed or self-aware
generation, and citation precision holds at unity anyway.

\subsection{The Evaluation Harness as a Failure Surface}
\label{ssec:harness}

Every result above was, until recently, guarded by a regression check of the
form \texttt{python3 eval/run\_X.py >/dev/null \&\& echo PASS}. That check
confirms only that the script did not raise an exception. It asserts nothing
about the numbers the script computes: a script silently reporting 0.00 for
every metric still prints \texttt{PASS}. The harness therefore verified
\emph{well-formedness} and not \emph{correctness} --- precisely the distinction
this paper argues is the difference between a statistical control and a
guarantee, reproduced inside our own instrumentation.

The evaluation scripts were converted so that each exposes a function
returning its metrics as structured values while still printing the
human-readable table it printed before; the tables in this section and the
assertions are computed by the same call, so they cannot diverge. Ninety-one
assertions now encode explicit numeric thresholds for every figure reported
above, together with the architectural invariants stated in Section~IV, tested
directly rather than through a metric: that no module under \texttt{verify/}
imports an inference, learned-similarity, or network library (checked by
walking the import graph); that verification is deterministic across
independently constructed instances; that no emitted answer contains an
unresolvable marker or a nonexistent provision under any of the four
repair~$\times$~support configurations; that V1, V2 and V4 strip rather than
annotate; and that V3 and V5 annotate without ever stripping.

Converting the harness also exposed drift between this paper and the code. Three

We note the limits of this exercise. Mutation coverage demonstrates that the
assertions detect the defects we thought to inject, which is the same
circularity the probe sets suffer from and which Section~\ref{ssec:evasion} describes; it is
evidence that the harness is not vacuous, not that it is complete.

\section{Expected Outcomes and Discussion}
\label{sec:outcomes}

\emph{The following concern components not yet implemented, and are expectations derived from the surveyed literature rather than measured results. Measured results appear in Section~\ref{sec:prelim}.}

Two claims that stood here in every prior revision are measured now rather than expected, and are reported in Section~\ref{ssec:dense} and Section~\ref{ssec:fusion}: hybrid retrieval outperforms both single-arm configurations, and absolute recall on a real corpus falls inside the 0.49--0.60 range \cite{rustamov2025} this paper cited as a realistic reference point before having its own number to compare. Both are left here as a record of what this section predicted before Section~\ref{sec:prelim} measured it, per the fix-history practice stated in Section~\ref{sec:limitations} -- moving an entry from expected to measured, not deleting it.

The distinctive expectation concerns citation precision. Because the verifier removes any claim whose citation does not resolve, post-verifier citation precision is unity as a matter of construction rather than of measurement. The scientifically interesting quantities are therefore the \emph{pre}-verifier hallucination rate, which measures how much work the verifier is doing, and citation recall, which measures what the guarantee costs in coverage. A system that achieves precision 1.0 by abstaining from every query is trivially correct and useless; reporting coverage alongside precision is what makes the result meaningful.

We also expect the recodification mapping to be a disproportionately visible contribution in practice. Queries phrased in terms of familiar IPC sections remain common, and a system that silently answers under superseded law is more dangerous than one that declines, because the answer is fluent and plausible.

\section{Limitations and Threats to Validity}
\label{sec:limitations}

Several limitations warrant statement in advance. First, and most importantly, the verifier guarantees that citations \emph{resolve}, not that they are \emph{apposite}. Section~\ref{sec:prelim} quantifies how far the advisory support screen narrows this gap and where it stops: gross topical mismatch is detected at flagging precision 1.000 and recall 0.818, but topically adjacent misattribution is provably outside the reach of a lexical method, because adjacent provisions share subject-matter vocabulary by construction and differ in grammatical role. Section~\ref{ssec:xrefsupport} shows the same boundary defeats a graph-based repair for the same reason, and reports a second bound: on citations to provisions that are not semantically self-contained, V5's flagging precision is 0.500 rather than 1.000, improved to 0.667 by consulting the citation graph but not restored. Closing that residue requires semantic entailment judgement, which we deliberately keep outside the deterministic layer --- admitting a model there would forfeit the property that makes the layer trustworthy --- and measure instead through human evaluation restricted to the tractable question of whether the cited text supports the claim.

Second, our corpus is bounded to Central statutes and Supreme Court judgments. State legislation, High Court jurisprudence at scale, and multilingual queries are out of scope, and abstention on such queries is the intended rather than degraded behaviour.

Third, evaluation without legal experts constrains what can be assessed. We mitigate this by relying on community relevance judgments for retrieval and by restricting human evaluation to support verification, which non-lawyers can perform by reading the source.

Fourth, the recodification mapping is only as reliable as the correspondence tables from which it is constructed, and section-to-section correspondence across the 2023 recodification is not always one-to-one.

Fifth, the guarantee is bounded by the parser: a citation the parser fails to extract is never checked, and Section~\ref{ssec:evasion} documents twelve such defects found across four rounds of adversarial audit and since repaired -- the third and fourth rounds found not by auditing the parser directly but by inspecting genuinely generated live text (the demo build, then the live-generation experiment itself), which is itself evidence that the first two rounds, however thorough, were not exhaustive, and that real generation keeps finding what deliberate probing did not. We do not claim the audit was exhaustive even now, and the guarantee should be read as holding over the citation forms the parser demonstrably covers rather than over legal citation in general.

Sixth, partially addressed since the previous revision. The verifier/repair/support/normaliser probe sets remain small, hand-authored, and on the 35-provision development corpus by design (Section~\ref{sec:limitations} originally raised this to flag that retrieval figures in particular were saturated and would not survive contact with a real corpus). That specific concern is now addressed: Section~\ref{ssec:realcorpus} reports retrieval, quotation-checking, and cross-reference results on a 3{,}263-provision real corpus instead, and BM25 Recall@1 falls from the toy corpus's meaningless 1.000 to a real 0.375, below rather than merely inside the reference range this paper had cited. What remains unaddressed is the probe sets themselves, which function as regression baselines rather than independent validation of the deterministic checks -- a different claim from the one about retrieval saturation, and one this revision does not touch.

Seventh, the confidence gate is calibrated rather than deterministic, and we do not claim otherwise: it is a tuned threshold on a retrieval score and can both refuse an answerable question and accept an unanswerable one. Its reported performance rests on sixteen unanswerable queries we wrote ourselves, split eight for tuning and eight for reporting, which establishes that the calibration procedure is sound and not that the gate performs at that level on a realistic query distribution. \emph{Partially addressed since the previous revision:} the concern that a toy-corpus threshold might not transfer to a realistic corpus is no longer speculative -- Section~\ref{sssec:confidence-real} reports that it demonstrably does not (the deployed $\tau_c=0.305$ reproducibly answers an out-of-scope query against the real corpus), and reports an independently recalibrated $\tau_r=1.088$ that restores balanced accuracy to 0.938 on a held-out split of the same size and construction. The ``author-written'' concern is itself now partly addressed: an out-of-sample check against eight genuinely externally-sourced queries (real page titles from a public legal Q\&A platform, not composed by this project) measures a correct-abstention rate of 0.750, a real degradation from the perfect 1.000 on the author-written split, with both failures traced to specific, explained causes rather than left as an unexplained gap. What remains unaddressed is narrower than before but still real: eight queries from one platform is a small, single-source sample, not a large adversarial corpus, and the case this gate is least equipped for --- a question whose answer is partially present, where neither a confident answer nor a refusal is correct --- is absent from every probe set built so far.

Eighth, \emph{partially addressed since the previous revision}, on the real corpus and the components built on top of it. India Code, the intended statutory source, blocked automated access outright; Indian Kanoon was substituted, and Section~\ref{ssec:realcorpus} reports what was checked and what was not. The "structural sweeps" this entry previously described as a stated gap are no longer merely aspirational: a systematic pass over all 3{,}263 provisions (not spot-checks, not reactive probing) found and fixed two further defects affecting twelve provisions, and a repeat of that same sweep -- re-run rather than assumed sufficient after one pass -- found four more (glued clause text, heading-embedded amendment history, whole-section loss where the operative rule lives only in the heading, and superseded text leaking into a current section's body), bringing the total to eleven parser defects found and fixed across five rounds of audit, one of them a correction of this paper's own prior conclusion. That the second sweep found anything at all, using the same method against the same corpus with no new heuristic, is itself the finding: a structural sweep is not self-certifying after one clean pass. What remains a stated gap is unchanged: Indian Evidence Act \S67A, whose source heading is itself malformed; state- and union-territory-specific amendment tables sharing markup with genuinely current schedule content, confirmed present but not reliably separable from it without risking real law; and scraped section text is still not individually legally reviewed at the provision level -- the structural sweep finds anomalies a human reader would notice (truncation, duplication, superseded text), not silent substantive inaccuracy in otherwise well-formed text. The dense-retrieval ablation (Section~\ref{ssec:dense}) found InLegalBERT's raw embeddings underperform a general-purpose retrieval-tuned encoder, which is evidence about the specific comparison run (an MLM-only checkpoint against a contrastively-fine-tuned one) and should not be read as a claim that legal-domain pre-training does not help retrieval in general -- a domain-adapted encoder that also received retrieval-objective fine-tuning is the comparison this result motivates and was not run.

Ninth, \emph{partially addressed since the previous revision}. The pipeline (Section~\ref{ssec:pipeline}) is no longer only wired against a scripted stand-in: Section~\ref{ssec:live-generation} reports a real, independently invoked generator run end to end through the deployed gate, retrieval, and verifier. What remains genuinely unrun is the specific ablation this project's abstract and introduction motivate as the headline result -- a large, externally-sourced, adversarial query set, not the still-project-authored sixteen gold queries -- and the pipeline-level tests continue to use a scripted stand-in for that reason: a live API call could never back a number this paper claims as reproducible without a paid credential (Section~\ref{sec:prelim}'s reproducibility rule), so the scripted fake remains what CI actually runs, alongside the live round as a separate, disclosed data point.

Tenth, on the instrumentation itself. The regression harness now asserts
explicit numeric thresholds for every figure reported in
Section~\ref{sec:prelim} and tests the architectural invariants directly, and
Section~\ref{ssec:harness} validates it by mutation; this addresses a defect
under which the harness confirmed only that its scripts did not crash. Two
residual weaknesses should be stated plainly. The mutation set was authored by
us, so it establishes that the assertions catch the defects we anticipated,
which is the same circularity the probe sets exhibit. And the assertions pin
published figures to values recomputed from the development corpus, so they
detect divergence between the paper and the code but not error common to both:
they will report that a recodification mapping's verification flag has been
changed without evidence, and they will not report that a mapping is legally
wrong.

\section{Conclusion}

Every legal RAG system in the surveyed literature controls hallucination statistically, and none reaches zero. This paper has argued that the residual is structural rather than incidental: a probabilistic mechanism cannot supply a guarantee. We have proposed \textit{Is It Legal?}, whose deterministic citation verifier makes citation faithfulness a property of the architecture rather than of the model, and have situated three supporting contributions — recodification-aware corpus unification, calibrated abstention, and standardised evaluation — against specific documented gaps in the literature.

The implementation reported here supports the argument substantially further than a preliminary one. Every architectural stage now exists in code and is wired end to end (Section~\ref{ssec:pipeline}); retrieval, fusion, and reranking are measured on a real 3{,}263-provision corpus rather than a saturated 35-provision one (Sections~\ref{ssec:realcorpus}--\ref{ssec:fusion}); and the verifier's checks fire correctly on planted faults without disturbing clean answers. The recodification diagnostic shows a failure mode --- lexical retrieval preferring repealed law to current law in two cases out of three --- that conventional retrieval metrics do not expose and that the verification layer corrects. The quotation-checking negative result is a reminder that the deterministic layer must be designed with the semantics of legal citation in mind rather than borrowed wholesale from generic text matching, and the dense-retrieval negative result is the same reminder applied to embeddings: domain-adapted pre-training without a retrieval objective did not transfer. What remains --- and it is the single largest remaining piece, not one item among several --- is running the wired pipeline against a real language model and measuring the pre/post-verifier hallucination comparison this paper's central claim has, until that is done, only argued for rather than shown end to end.

Future work, beyond running the pipeline against a real generator, includes traversal of a combined statute--judgment citation graph with authority-weighted reranking; a domain-adapted encoder that also receives retrieval-objective fine-tuning, motivated directly by Section~\ref{ssec:dense}'s finding that domain pre-training alone was insufficient; temporal reasoning over amendment history to answer questions of the form ``was X lawful in 2015?''; and multilingual query support for Indian languages.

\section*{Ethical Considerations}

This system is a legal research and citation tool, not a source of legal advice, and is presented to users as such. The access-to-justice case for reducing the cost of locating authority is strong, but so is the risk that a fluent system is over-relied upon by users unable to evaluate it. The abstention mechanism, the visible provenance of every claim, and the prominence of the disclaimer are design responses to that risk rather than afterthoughts. Source materials are public-domain government and judicial documents; no personally identifying case data beyond what is already published by the courts is introduced.
