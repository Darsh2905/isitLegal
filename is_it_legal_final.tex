\maketitle

\begin{abstract}
Large language models answer legal questions fluently, but many of their citations are fabricated: hallucination rates on verifiable legal questions run 58--88\% even at temperature zero, and models cannot reliably detect their own errors. In India the problem is compounded by the July 2024 recodification, which replaced the Indian Penal Code, Code of Criminal Procedure and Indian Evidence Act with the Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita, Bharatiya Nagarik Suraksha Sanhita and Bharatiya Sakshya Adhiniyam and renumbered their sections. Every Indian legal RAG system we surveyed reduces hallucination with a statistical or human check, and none removes it. We present Is It Legal?, whose citation verifier is built only from regular expressions, database lookups and string matching, so that it performs no inference and cannot itself hallucinate. The verifier guarantees \emph{resolution}, not support: every citation that survives it names a real provision of a 3,275-provision statutory corpus or a real judgment of a 110-judgment Supreme Court corpus, every quotation is verbatim, and any reference-shaped text the parser cannot read is removed rather than passed. On real statutory text it strips all 39 planted faults across two adversarial rounds without removing any of 38 correct citations. Across \RtwoAnswers{} answers written by three independently invoked models for \RtwoQueries{} queries, \RtwoQueriesAila{} of them externally authored AILA fact situations, verification raises citation resolution from \RtwoGroundedPre{} to 1.000 for retrieval-grounded answers and from \RtwoUngroundedPre{} to 1.000 for ungrounded ones, keeping \RtwoGroundedCov{} and \RtwoUngroundedCov{} of sentences, at a median cost of \LatencyMedian\,ms per answer. Support is measured separately, on an author-labelled sample: \SupGroundedPrec{} of surviving grounded sentences are fully supported by what they cite, against \SupUngroundedPrec{} of ungrounded ones, and the advisory support screen catches \SupVfiveCaught{} unsupported sentences at a flagging precision of only \SupVfiveFlagPrec{}. On the AILA 2019 statute-retrieval benchmark our untuned retrieval stack places mid-table (MAP 0.078 against a best published 0.157), while on our own questions it reaches an MRR@10 of 0.821. We report each of these alongside its sample size and confidence interval.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Legal research in India is slowed by bureaucracy and fragmented court records, which makes it expensive. A lawyer or an ordinary person asking whether an act is legal needs to know which statutes or previous rulings support or contradict that conclusion, and needs that answer in a form they can verify before acting on it.

When legal questions are given to large language models (LLMs), the answers sound confident and authoritative, especially to people who do not practise law. The problem is that a large fraction of those answers are wrong, and the damage can be serious because citations can be fabricated outright. Dahl et al. \cite{dahl2024} queried several LLMs with verifiable questions about randomly selected United States federal cases and found that 58\% of GPT-4's answers and 88\% of Llama-2's were hallucinated at temperature zero. The models cannot flag their own answers as wrong, and in what-if scenarios they often accept and build on a user's premise even when it has no legal standing.

Three features of the Indian legal system make the problem worse:

\begin{enumerate}
\item Exact-reference density. Legal questions are entity-heavy and depend on exact references: a query about ``Section 138 of the Negotiable Instruments Act'' must be matched exactly, and Section 139 is a different provision with a different answer.
\item Statutory volatility. In July 2024 the Indian Penal Code, Code of Criminal Procedure and Indian Evidence Act were replaced by the Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita (BNS), Bharatiya Nagarik Suraksha Sanhita (BNSS) and Bharatiya Sakshya Adhiniyam (BSA) respectively \cite{dixit2025}. Sections were rearranged and renumbered, so any system trained or indexed on the pre-2023 codes is now systematically misaligned with current law.
\item Document length and structure. Supreme Court and High Court judgments can run to tens of thousands of words, which saturates keyword statistics and exceeds LLM context and embedding limits.
\end{enumerate}

Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG) \cite{lewis2020} is the standard response: it provides provenance, since answers can be traced to retrieved evidence, and updatability, since swapping the index updates the system's knowledge without retraining. The evidence in Section~\ref{sec:related}, however, shows that grounding alone is not enough. In published Indian legal RAG systems, hallucination falls steadily as the architecture becomes more sophisticated --- from 30.6\% for ungrounded generation to 16.9\% for vanilla RAG and 6.3\% for a graph-augmented adversarial architecture \cite{bindra2026} --- but no architecture reaches zero, because in every case the control is statistical or relies on human review, and so cannot guarantee a correct answer.

\subsection{Contributions}

The system is aimed at the gap that remains:

\begin{itemize}
\item C1 --- A deterministic, fail-closed citation verifier. A model-free post-processing layer that guarantees \emph{resolution}: every citation marker must point to a retrieved chunk, every cited provision or case must exist in the corpus, every quotation must occur verbatim in its source, and any text that looks like a legal reference but cannot be parsed is removed. Whether a resolved citation also \emph{supports} its claim is screened by an advisory check and measured separately (Section~\ref{ssec:support}).
\item C2 --- A statutory corpus with a recodification mapping, and a judgment corpus used as a verification target. A bidirectional mapping for IPC$\leftrightarrow$BNS, CrPC$\leftrightarrow$BNSS and IEA$\leftrightarrow$BSA built from published correspondence tables and corroborated against a second, independent table (Section~\ref{ssec:provenance}); and 110 Supreme Court judgments against which case citations are checked.
\item C3 --- A calibrated abstention gate: an external confidence threshold tuned on a development split and evaluated on deliberately unanswerable queries, so that the system declines to answer when its corpus cannot support an answer.
\item C4 --- An evaluation protocol that reports citation resolution, citation support and coverage separately, each with its sample size and confidence interval, and includes a community benchmark (AILA 2019). RAGAS scoring and the IL-TUR benchmark, also planned, have not yet been run.
\item C5 --- Index-time cross-reference resolution: statutory references are resolved when the corpus is indexed and stored as a citation graph, so provisions whose text only points elsewhere can still be reached.
\end{itemize}

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}

The literature falls into five threads that together explain the design space.

\subsection{Thread A: Foundations of Retrieval-Augmented Generation}

Lewis et al. \cite{lewis2020} describe RAG as a general recipe that pairs a parametric generator with a non-parametric index. Karpukhin et al. \cite{karpukhin2020} showed that Dense Passage Retrieval (DPR), a dual encoder trained with in-batch negatives, clearly outperforms BM25 on open-domain retrieval. This matters for law because dense retrievers perform worse on rare tokens --- especially the section numbers and act names that dominate legal queries. That empirical complementarity is the root of the case for hybrid retrieval.

Sun \cite{sun2025} built a system that applies hybrid retrieval. AH-RAG combines keyword search (BM25) with vector search (BGE-small/FAISS) and computes a separate weighting for every query according to how complex the question is. On a standard benchmark (Natural Questions), combining the two raised retrieval accuracy (Recall@20) from 52.4\% to 67.7\% while making retrieval only 4.8\% slower. Shifting the mix dynamically added 1--3 points, and passing confidence scores to the LLM added 0.5--1.5 points of exact match. The two lessons are that no fixed ratio between search methods works --- the balance must change with the query --- and that passing a retrieval confidence score to the LLM reduces hallucination.

\subsection{Thread B: Adaptive Retrieval and Long Documents}

Asai et al. \cite{asai2023} train a language model to emit reflection tokens that control its retrieval behaviour --- whether to retrieve, whether a passage is relevant, whether the output is supported and whether the response is useful --- and report gains in factuality and accuracy, including for long-form generation. Reflection tokens are beyond the scope of this system, but the work justifies two of our components: an abstention check and post-generation support checking. Our deterministic verifier is essentially a hard-coded analogue of Self-RAG's output critique.

Sarthi et al. \cite{sarthi2024} discuss the mismatch between flat chunk retrieval and document-level questions. RAPTOR recursively clusters and summarises chunks into a tree whose upper nodes hold more general summaries. Combined with GPT-4, this improved accuracy on the QuALITY long-document benchmark by roughly 20 points, which directly motivates our retrieval design for long judgments.

\subsection{Thread C: Legal-Domain Language Models and Indian Resources}

Chalkidis et al. \cite{chalkidis2020} released LEGAL-BERT, pre-trained on legal text, with clear gains over generic BERT that show sub-domain adaptation matters. Zheng et al. \cite{zheng2021} asked when domain pre-training helps, and showed with CaseLawBERT that the gains are largest when the task is domain-specific and difficult.

Paul et al. \cite{paul2023} applied this to Indian law, pre-training the InLegalBERT and InCaseLawBERT checkpoints on 5.4 million Indian court documents from 1950--2019. InLegalBERT outperforms earlier general and foreign legal-domain models, and the authors conclude that Indian legal text differs enough in convention, drafting style and register that even foreign legal-domain models are suboptimal.

For corpora and benchmarks, Malik et al. \cite{malik2021} released ILDC, roughly 35,000 annotated Supreme Court judgments, with the Court Judgment Prediction and Explanation task, documenting a gap between model and expert explanations. Joshi et al. \cite{joshi2024} brought the field together in IL-TUR, which covers rhetorical role labelling, named-entity recognition, judgment and bail prediction, summarisation and --- most importantly for us --- legal statute identification and prior case retrieval. Their baselines show that no model, GPT-3.5 and GPT-4 included, performs well across the tasks. The FIRE AILA track \cite{bhattacharya2019} is the Indian shared task built for retrieval, with relevance judgments for precedent and statute retrieval; we evaluate on it in Section~\ref{ssec:aila}.

Patil et al. \cite{patil2025} built BNS Mitra, which maps informal descriptions of events to BNS sections. LLaMA-2 rewrites the user's query in legal terms, and the rewritten query is embedded and matched against a FAISS index of BNS chunks produced by a recursive character splitter. On 500 lawyer-verified queries the system reached 87\% accuracy, 12 points above a non-RAG baseline. Accuracy was higher on straightforward queries and fell on complex ones where several sections could apply, and the authors' own examples include questionable output, such as a bodily-assault scenario mapped to mischief provisions. The lessons are that rewriting queries into legal terminology helps; that character-based chunking of statutes is a defect, correctable by chunking at section boundaries; and that multi-section applicability is a hard case that has to be evaluated section by section.

Dixit et al. \cite{dixit2025} presented Nyaya-Sathi, built around the recodification. BNS sections with metadata are embedded into ChromaDB, retrieved sections are passed to an LLM API, and a post-processing stage checks the generated citations against the database and removes unverified output. They report Precision@1 of 0.85 against 0.60 without RAG, citation accuracy of 0.92 against 0.70, and hallucination reduced from 0.25 to 0.05. The authors note that the verification stage involves confirmation by human experts, and list as limitations the statute-only coverage, the absence of case law and cross-references, and a small evaluation set.

Bolla et al. \cite{bolla2026} built the architecture closest to our retrieval system, applied to the Constitution of India: each article is indexed both as an InLegalBERT dense vector and in a BM25 lexical index, the two rankings are merged by Reciprocal Rank Fusion (RRF), a MiniLM cross-encoder reranks the result, and an LLM summarises it. They report a hybrid F1 of 89.4\%, with hybrid retrieval beating both single-arm configurations in precision, recall and F1 --- a clean confirmation of the sparse-plus-dense-plus-rerank consensus on Indian legal text specifically. The system has no abstention mechanism and reports no hallucination metrics.

Bindra et al. \cite{bindra2026} built the most ambitious Indian system, indexing over 10,000 Supreme Court judgments together with the Constitution and the CrPC both as dense vectors and as a citation graph. Constrained multi-hop traversal recovers related precedents that share no vocabulary with the query. An adversarial module inspired by GANs retrieves supporting and opposing arguments, and a discriminative evaluator critiques biased, one-sided or unsupported output. Accuracy rises from 63\% to 77\%; groundedness from 0.43 (LLM-only) and 0.73 (vanilla RAG) to 0.95; precedent recall from 0.56 and 0.72 to 0.87; hallucination falls from 30.6\% and 16.9\% to 6.3\%; and citation accuracy rises from 1.8\% and 67.4\% to 96.1\%, at the cost of latency rising from 2.5\,s to 15.3\,s. A case study on arrest powers under Section 41 CrPC shows that only the graph-augmented configuration surfaced the constitutional dimension (Articles 21 and 22) alongside the statute. Three implications follow: the 1.8\% citation accuracy of ungrounded generation is a striking Indian-law confirmation of Dahl et al.; citation-graph traversal is the strongest case for relational structure; and the remaining 6.3\% hallucination persists because the controls are statistical rather than deterministic.

Rustamov et al. \cite{rustamov2025} give the cleanest and most methodical study in this thread. Their subject is the Azerbaijani Tax Code, a different jurisdiction from ours, but it shares the structural problems of Indian statutes. Of 5,657 citizen question--answer pairs they kept 2,178 that are answerable from the Code's 240 articles alone, chunked at 300 tokens with a 60-token overlap after analysing the distribution of article lengths (mean 1,500 tokens, median 670). They benchmark two sparse and three dense retrievers across six IR metrics: the best single retriever (BGE-m3) reaches only 0.49 recall@100, weighted hybrid BM25+BGE-m3 raises this to 0.60, and cross-encoder reranking improves NDCG from 0.39 to 0.44 while lowering MRR from 0.60 to 0.39, because it fails to promote the first relevant document. Their error analysis is the paper's most valuable contribution: retrieval failures concentrate on articles that are not semantically self-contained because they cross-reference other articles, and the authors argue for knowledge-graph structure to resolve them.

\subsection{Thread E: Hallucination Measurement and RAG Evaluation}

Dahl et al. \cite{dahl2024}, discussed in Section~I, supply both the motivating measurement and a typology that distinguishes fidelity to the input, to the training data and to legal fact. Es et al. \cite{es2023} introduced RAGAS, which evaluates RAG pipelines without gold answers by decomposing quality into faithfulness, answer relevance and context relevance, each scored by structured LLM prompting. RAGAS has a known limitation: LLM-as-judge scores are themselves fallible and cannot be guaranteed hallucination-free. This is precisely why a deterministic citation check is needed alongside any such metric.

\subsection{Convergent Findings}

Five conclusions recur independently enough across the surveyed literature to be treated as established:

\begin{enumerate}
\item Hybrid retrieval is a consensus, not a design choice. Every study comparing sparse, dense and hybrid configurations --- AH-RAG on open-domain QA \cite{sun2025}, Rustamov et al. on statutory text \cite{rustamov2025}, Constitutional Compass on Indian constitutional text \cite{bolla2026} --- found hybrid configurations better.
\item Retrieval, not generation, is the recall bottleneck. A 0.49 single-model recall ceiling \cite{rustamov2025} and the precedent-recall gains from graph traversal \cite{bindra2026} both show that the hard problem is finding the right authority for a query.
\item Grounding reduces but does not eliminate hallucination. The progression 30.6\% $\rightarrow$ 16.9\% $\rightarrow$ 6.3\% \cite{bindra2026}, and 0.25 $\rightarrow$ 0.05 with partly manual verification \cite{dixit2025}, show a consistent decrease, but no surveyed system reaches zero and none implements fully deterministic, automatic verification.
\item Domain adaptation compounds. LegalBERT improves on BERT \cite{chalkidis2020}; CaseLawBERT clarifies when \cite{zheng2021}; InLegalBERT improves on both for Indian tasks \cite{paul2023}.
\item Statutory cross-references defeat flat semantic retrieval. Articles that merely reference other articles are effectively invisible to embeddings, as error analysis \cite{rustamov2025} and graph-traversal gains \cite{bindra2026} both confirm.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Research Gaps}

From the survey we isolate five gaps, each mapped in Table~\ref{tab:gaps} to a contribution.

G1 --- No deterministic citation verification. In all six applied systems, hallucination control is statistical (adversarial evaluators, confidence weighting) or relies on manual oversight. No system checks automatically and deterministically that every citation maps to a retrieved source, that quoted text matches its source, or that cited provisions carry their current repeal status.

G2 --- No system handles the recodification across statutes. BNS Mitra and Nyaya-Sathi cover only the BNS; Constitutional Compass only the Constitution; Dual-Layer covers judgments and the CrPC. Recodification is named as a primary motivation \cite{dixit2025}, but none of the surveyed systems implements a bidirectional old--new section mapping.

G3 --- No calibrated abstention in legal RAG. Confidence mechanisms exist in open-domain QA \cite{sun2025}, and recall ceilings of 0.49--0.60 \cite{rustamov2025} show why a legal application needs one, but none of the systems can report that it lacks the authority to answer. Since models cannot evaluate themselves \cite{dahl2024}, the gate has to be external.

G4 --- Incomparable, non-standard evaluation. The applied systems use their own metrics and private query sets; none reports results on a community dataset such as AILA or IL-TUR, and none separates whether a citation exists from whether it supports the claim, which makes cross-system comparison unreliable.

G5 --- Statutory cross-reference structure unexploited. Rustamov et al. identify the problem but defer graphs to future work; Bindra et al. build graphs over judgment citations but not over statutory cross-references.

\section{Proposed System}

\subsection{Corpus Construction}

\subsubsection{Statutes} The statutory corpus holds 3,275 provisions of ten Central enactments: the IPC, BNS, CrPC, BNSS, IEA, BSA, the Negotiable Instruments Act, the Information Technology Act, the Indian Contract Act and the Constitution. The text is taken from Indian Kanoon, not from an official source: India Code, the intended source, refused every automated request. Each provision is stored at section level (sub-sections are resolved from the section text, Section~\ref{ssec:verifier}) with its act, section number, marginal note and repeal status. Section~\ref{ssec:provenance} measures how faithfully this text reproduces the official Gazette text where an official copy was obtainable.

\subsubsection{Recodification mapping} The IPC$\leftrightarrow$BNS, CrPC$\leftrightarrow$BNSS and IEA$\leftrightarrow$BSA correspondences are parsed from the comparative tables published by the Uttar Pradesh Police: 1,086 section-level pairs, giving 1,084 of the 1,264 repealed provisions a recorded successor. These are a state police department's tables, not an Act of Parliament; we mark them as sourced and corroborate the BNS$\leftrightarrow$IPC pairs against a second, independent table in Section~\ref{ssec:provenance}.

\subsubsection{Judgments} 110 Supreme Court judgments are taken from Indian Kanoon, the source ILDC \cite{malik2021} was itself built from, with case name, equivalent citations, bench, date and statutes referred. They serve as the verification target for case citations (Section~\ref{ssec:verifier}); they are not yet retrieved for generation.

\subsubsection{Normalisation} Citation strings are canonicalised to one internal form --- ``Sec.\ 420 IPC'', ``s.420 of the Indian Penal Code'' and ``section 420, IPC'' all resolve to IPC\S420 --- so that a citation generated by the LLM can be compared with the corpus.

\subsubsection{Chunking} Statutes are chunked one section per chunk, the natural unit of citation, with the parent Act attached for context; long sections are scored over overlapping 150-word windows. Judgments are chunked by paragraph with parent-document context. This avoids the arbitrary splits of recursive character splitting \cite{patil2025} and follows the length-informed approach of \cite{rustamov2025}.

\subsection{Retrieval Architecture}

At query time the system executes seven stages.

Stage 1 --- Query understanding. In the present system this stage is minimal: the query passes to retrieval as written. Entity extraction and query rewriting \cite{patil2025} are left to future work.

Stage 2 --- Hybrid retrieval. Snowball-stemmed BM25 and dense retrieval run in parallel, each returning its top 20, and are merged by Reciprocal Rank Fusion ($k=60$). Two embedding models are compared: InLegalBERT \cite{paul2023} as the domain-adapted arm and a general model (all-MiniLM-L6-v2) as the control.

Stage 3 --- Cross-encoder reranking. The fused top 20 is reranked to the top 5, each candidate scored by its best passage window; a repealed provision's recorded successor is then moved ahead of it.

Stage 4 --- Confidence gate (C3). If the confidence score falls below a threshold $\tau$ tuned on a development split, the system abstains with an explicit insufficient-authority response rather than generating an answer.

Stage 5 --- Generation. An instruction-tuned model generates the answer under a prompt that forbids outside knowledge, requires an inline citation marker for every claim, and requires verbatim text inside quotation marks.

Stage 6 --- Citation verification (C1), Section~\ref{ssec:verifier}.

Stage 7 --- Response assembly. The verified answer is returned with source cards showing the corpus text behind each claim, a recodification note wherever a repealed provision is cited, and a disclaimer that the system is a research and citation tool, not a substitute for legal advice.

\subsection{Design Invariants}
\label{ssec:invariants}

Five properties carry the paper's central claim, and the regression harness tests each directly (Section~\ref{ssec:harness}):

\begin{enumerate}
\item[I1] No model inside the verifier. No module of the verification layer imports an inference, learned-similarity or network library.
\item[I2] Resolution is 1.0 by construction. A claim whose marker, provision, case or quotation fails to resolve is stripped or repaired, never left in with a caveat.
\item[I3] Repair re-verifies. A repaired sentence passes through the same checks as any other.
\item[I4] The support screen is advisory. It annotates and never strips.
\item[I5] Coverage is reported with every resolution figure, so that a verifier that strips everything cannot look good.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{The Deterministic Citation Verifier}
\label{ssec:verifier}

The verifier is the paper's main contribution. It uses no machine-learned component and makes no probabilistic judgment, so it cannot hallucinate. It runs sentence by sentence; a sentence boundary is never placed inside a quotation, so a multi-sentence quotation is checked whole.

\begin{itemize}
\item V1 --- markers. Every citation marker, in any of the bracket forms models emit (square, lenticular or full-width brackets around $n$), must index a retrieved chunk.
\item V2 --- existence. Every statutory citation must resolve in the corpus: with its act named; with the act implied (``Section 41'' is placed in the nearest act named before it, then the act of the cited chunk, then the act last cited --- one candidate only); and with sub-section pinpoints, which resolve only if the labels occur as labels in the section's own text. A section cited to an act outside the corpus is stripped. Case names (``A v.\ B'') and Supreme Court reporter citations (SCC, SCR, SCALE, AIR, SCC OnLine) must resolve in the judgment corpus or occur in a retrieved chunk; a case name and a reporter citation that name different judgments are stripped.
\item V3 --- status. A repealed provision is annotated with its successor(s); not stripped.
\item V4 --- quotations. Every quoted span must occur verbatim, token for token, in a cited source, or be a faithful marked elision of one (every segment verbatim and in order, no omitted stretch longer than 40 tokens, and no negation, exception, condition or alternative omitted). A quotation with no verbatim source to check against is stripped.
\item Residual scan (fail-closed). Independently of the parser, any span that looks like a legal reference --- a provision word followed by a number or label, a section symbol, an act name next to a number, an ordinal section, a case name, a reporter citation, an unreadable marker --- whose number no extractor claimed is treated as unverifiable, and its sentence is stripped.
\item V5 --- support (advisory). An IDF-weighted lexical overlap between the claim and its cited provision, annotating weak support without stripping (I4).
\end{itemize}

Let $A$ be the generated answer, $R = \{r_1, \dots, r_k\}$ the retrieved chunks, $M$ the metadata and judgment databases.

\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\STATE \textbf{Input:} answer $A$, retrieved set $R$, databases $M$
\STATE \textbf{Output:} verified answer $A'$, verification report $V$
\FOR{each sentence $s$ of $A$ (never split inside a quotation)}
    \STATE apply repair rules R1--R3 to $s$ (Section~\ref{ssec:repair})
    \IF{a marker in $s$ indexes no chunk in $R$}
        \STATE strip $s$
    \ENDIF
    \FOR{each reference $c$ in $s$ (statutory, bare, case, reporter)}
        \IF{$c$ does not resolve in $M$ (or, for a case, in $R$)}
            \STATE strip $s$
        \ELSIF{$c$ is repealed}
            \STATE annotate with its successor
        \ENDIF
    \ENDFOR
    \IF{a reference-shaped span in $s$ was claimed by no extractor}
        \STATE strip $s$
    \ENDIF
    \FOR{each quoted span $q$ in $s$}
        \IF{$q$ is not verbatim in a cited source and not a faithful elision}
            \STATE strip $s$
        \ENDIF
    \ENDFOR
    \STATE annotate weak support (V5); log every intervention to $V$
\ENDFOR
\RETURN $A'$, $V$
\end{algorithmic}

Three properties follow. First, citation \emph{resolution} on $A'$ is 1.0 by construction: any citation that fails to resolve is removed before the answer is emitted. This is not a claim that the citations \emph{support} their claims; a real provision attached to the wrong claim resolves, and only the advisory screen V5 looks for it. Second, the verifier is cheap: it performs string matching and database lookups (Section~\ref{ssec:latency}). Third, it is auditable: the report $V$ records every intervention.

The cost is coverage. Stripping unverifiable claims reduces completeness, and the fail-closed residual scan trades a little more coverage for closing the parser's blind spots. Both costs are measured, not assumed.

\subsection{Evidence-Bounded Repair}
\label{ssec:repair}

Stripping alone deletes recoverable content, so three repair rules run before verification, each admissible only when the evidence for the repair is already in the corpus, and each followed by the full set of checks (I3):

\begin{itemize}
\item R1 --- a marker that indexes no chunk is repointed to the retrieved chunk holding the provision the sentence names.
\item R2 --- a citation to a repealed provision with a unique recorded successor is rewritten to the successor, keeping the original in parentheses. Enumerations and split sections are left to V3, which names every successor.
\item R3 --- a marker that points at a different retrieved provision from the one the sentence names is repointed to the named one; if the named provision was not retrieved, the mismatch is flagged instead.
\end{itemize}

Fabricated provisions and quotations are never repaired: repairing them would mean inventing text.

\subsection{Cross-Reference Resolution (C5)}

Following \cite{rustamov2025}, references between sections (``punishment as provided in Section X'') are identified at indexing time and stored as links, so that a section that depends on another can be reached through it. On the real corpus the graph holds 1,938 edges and marks 248 provisions as largely dependent on others. A combined statute--judgment graph would further support ``cases interpreting this section'' expansion and authority-weighted reranking, following \cite{bindra2026}.

\subsection{Metric Definitions}

Retrieval. Recall@$k$, MRR@10 and nDCG@10 on our gold questions; on AILA, the track's own measures --- MAP, BPREF, reciprocal rank and P@10, as computed by trec\_eval --- over the official test queries. MRR and nDCG are both reported because of the reranking anomaly of \cite{rustamov2025}.

Citation resolution. The fraction of citations in an answer --- markers, statutory references, case names and reporter citations --- that resolve to the corpus, re-derived from the text by an audit independent of the verifier's own report. After verification this is 1.0 by construction (I2); before verification it measures how much work the verifier does.

Citation support. The fraction of surviving cited sentences whose cited source actually states what the sentence claims, judged on a fixed random sample (Section~\ref{ssec:support}). Resolution and support are different properties and are never reported under one name.

Coverage. The fraction of generated sentences that survive verification.

Abstention. Balanced accuracy over answerable and deliberately unanswerable queries, tuned on a development split and reported on a held-out split.

Uncertainty. Every proportion from a sample is reported with its count and a 95\% Wilson interval.

\section{Implementation and Results}

\subsection{Scope of the Measurements}

The implementation is complete end to end: normaliser, metadata store and recodification mapping, the verifier with the residual scan, three repair rules, the support screen, cross-reference resolver and citation graph, BM25 and dense retrievers, RRF, the cross-encoder reranker, the confidence gate and the generator. This section reports results on the real 3,275-provision corpus, the judgment corpus, a community benchmark and real model output. The earlier component experiments, run on a 35-provision development corpus of paraphrased provisions, are summarised in Appendix~\ref{app:dev}; they show that each mechanism behaves as designed, not how well it performs on real law.

Most query sets here are small and many are our own. The gold retrieval set has 16 questions, the abstention sets 16 and 8, and the probe sets were written alongside the components they test, so perfect scores on them are closer to training performance than to independent validation. Where a result rests on such a set we say so and give its interval; the more independent evidence comes from the externally authored AILA queries, three independently invoked models and the labelled support sample.

Round 1 fixed five defects: a correct pinpoint such as ``Article 19(1)(a)'' was stripped because the corpus stores whole sections (it now resolves only if the cited labels occur as labels in the section text, so ``Article 19(2)(a)'', which the text only mentions, still fails); a section cited to an act outside the corpus produced no citation at all and passed; ``IPC 1860'' was read as section 1860; a marker written after the full stop was split off as its own sentence; and every elided quotation failed V4. Round 2 answered a reviewer's objection that a parser-based guarantee is fail-open: a citation the parser cannot read is never checked, and twelve such defects had already been found (Appendix~\ref{app:dev}). Attacking the parser again found the same class of hole within minutes. A bare ``Section 7777'' with no act name was never checked, although generators write bare sections constantly once an act has been named; so were ``s 7777 IPC'', ``IPC's section 7777'', ``Section 7777 (IPC)'', ``\S\S 302--7777'', ``Const \S7777'', ``the 7777th section'' and ``Section seven thousand''. ``Clause (9) of Article 22'' validated as Article 22, silently dropping the fabricated clause. Every case name and reporter citation passed unchecked. In the live answers, gpt-oss-20b wrote its own lenticular markers, which V1 never read. A sentence boundary inside a quotation let the tail of a multi-sentence quotation escape V4. Running the three-model round of Section~\ref{ssec:live} through the revised verifier found three more parser gaps, this time where the residual scan had stripped valid citations rather than missed fabricated ones: lists joined by ``or'' (``sections 494 or 495 of the IPC''), a mixed-act list (``Sections 198 CrPC and 219 BNSS''), and a pinpoint written before a bare section symbol. It also exposed false triggers of the scan itself: an act number inside a verbatim quotation (``Indian Penal Code (45 of 1860)'', which is Indian Kanoon's own text), a date beside an act name, and a ``Section(s)'' table heading. Each was fixed and added to the probe set as a matched pair. The probe set's check for corrupted output also exposed an older defect in R2: a citation matched with trailing whitespace made the rewrite glue the next word to its closing parenthesis (``(formerly IPC Section 302 )punishes''). The pre-revision code strips 1 of the resulting 22 faults and corrupts one of the 24 correct forms; the revised code strips all 22 and keeps all 24.

The probes were written alongside the fixes, which is the circularity the reviewer named, so the stronger evidence for the design is a counterfactual: running the residual scan alone behind the \emph{pre-revision} parser, it would have stripped every one of the 17 citation-form faults that parser missed. The remaining faults (a marker, three quotations) belong to V1 and V4. The scan does not need to anticipate a construct to catch it; it needs only to recognise that something looks like a citation and was not read.

\subsection{Quotation Verification: Exactness, and a Length-Dependent Flaw}
\label{ssec:quotes}

The quotation check originally used character-level fuzzy matching, which scored a meaning-inverting substitution (``procedure established by statute'' for ``by law'') at 95.9\%. Token-level scoring --- the longest contiguous run of quote tokens found in the source, divided by the quote's length --- with a threshold of $\theta = 0.95$ rejected that 18-token example (Appendix~\ref{app:dev}). A reviewer pointed out what that example could not show: one substituted word at either end of an $n$-token quotation scores $(n-1)/n$, which reaches 0.95 at $n = 20$, so every quotation of twenty or more tokens could have its first or last word changed and pass. \subsection{End-to-End Generation with Independent Models}
\label{ssec:live}

A first live round used one independently invoked model, gpt-oss-20b via the Groq API, on our sixteen gold questions. The gate passed ten; across them the model wrote 49 citations, one of which did not resolve (a real section attributed to the ``Bharatiya Samvidhan Adhiniyam'', an act that does not exist), and verification raised resolution from 0.980 (48/49) to 1.000 (46/46) with 97.2\% of sentences kept. That improvement rests on a single citation, so a second round was designed to remove three weaknesses at once: \emph{queries} --- AILA's fact situations, written by the track organisers, alongside the 16 gold questions; \emph{models} --- three independently invoked models, gpt-oss-20b, gpt-oss-120b and qwen3.8-27b, every chat model the provider offered this account; and \emph{conditions} --- grounded, through the deployed retrieval stack (with the gate's decision recorded but not enforced, so that the verifier sees as much output as possible), and ungrounded, where the model cites from its own knowledge, the configuration in which Bindra et al.\ measured 1.8\% citation accuracy. The provider's daily token quota stopped the run before it finished. \subsection{Resolution Is Not Support}
\label{ssec:support}

A resolved citation can still be the wrong citation: ``murder is punished under Section 300 of the Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita'', which gpt-oss-20b wrote ungrounded during setup, cites a real section that is not the murder provision. \subsection{Calibrated Abstention}
\label{ssec:gate}

Verification says nothing when a query's answer is not in the corpus at all: the retriever still returns its top-$k$, a generator still writes over it, and every citation can resolve while the answer is worthless. The gate declines to answer instead. It is a threshold on a retrieval score tuned on held-out data --- a statistical decision and a weaker guarantee than verification, which is why it sits in retrieval rather than verification. A raw BM25 score is not comparable across queries, because it scales with how many substantive terms a query has and how rare they are, so we normalise by the inverse document frequency the query brings:
\[
\mathrm{conf}(q) \;=\; \frac{\mathrm{bm25}_{\text{top-1}}(q)}{\sum_{t \in q} \mathrm{idf}(t)},
\]
the fraction of the evidence the query demanded that was actually found. A term missing from the corpus contributes maximum demand and zero score.

Sixteen unanswerable queries --- real questions of Indian law on tax, intellectual property, family law, company law, labour law and limitation, none covered by the corpus, and sharing vocabulary such as ``property'', ``bail'' and ``penalty'' with provisions that are --- and the sixteen gold questions were each split in half; thresholds are tuned on the dev half and reported on the held-out half (Table~\ref{tab:realconf}). The normalised signal generalises best, at $\tau_r = 1.077$: on the held-out half it answers 7 of 8 answerable questions and refuses all 8 unanswerable ones (balanced accuracy 0.938; with eight queries per class the interval is wide), while raw BM25 top-1 falls to 0.688. Stemming, which helps retrieval, hurts the gate: it raises the lexical overlap of out-of-scope questions too and cut held-out correct abstention from 1.000 to 0.625, so the gate keeps unstemmed BM25. On eight unanswerable questions taken verbatim from a public legal question-and-answer site, which we did not write, it abstains on six (0.750). The gate's threshold is a starting point, not a validated operating point; the genuinely hard case --- a question whose answer is partly in the corpus --- is not represented.

\subsection{Data Provenance and the Recodification Mapping}
\label{ssec:provenance}

Because the statutory text comes from Indian Kanoon rather than an official source, we measured it against the official text where one was obtainable: the Gazette of India (Extraordinary) of 25 December 2023, Part II, Section 1, which published the three new codes as enacted. For each provision we computed the fraction of its 8-token shingles that occur in the Gazette text, a measure that tolerates the Gazette's interleaved marginal notes while still failing changed wording. 1,040 of the 1,054 testable sections (98.7\%) reach 0.90 (BNS 348/353, BNSS 526/531, BSA 166/170), and every one below was read. Most differences are editorial: Indian Kanoon inlines act numbers such as ``(45 of 1950)'' that the Gazette prints in the margin, and tables extract in a different order. One is a transcription error: Indian Kanoon's BSA \S103 refers to the Indian Succession Act as ``(39 of 1995)'', where the Gazette's margin gives 39 of 1925. Because V4 is exact, a verbatim quotation of the Gazette text of such a section would be stripped against the corpus text --- a small coverage cost of an unofficial corpus. The repealed codes and the other Acts could not be checked: no official copy was obtainable.

The recodification mapping is built from correspondence tables published by the Uttar Pradesh Police, which are not an official schedule of the Acts. We compared its BNS$\leftrightarrow$IPC pairs with an independent table prepared by the Director of the Central Academy for Police Training, Bhopal. Of the 402 IPC sections both tables map, the two name the same BNS section(s) for 399 (0.993); the other three overlap partly (for example, IPC \S376 to BNS \S64 in one and \S\S64--65 in the other), and none disagrees outright. The BNSS$\leftrightarrow$CrPC and BSA$\leftrightarrow$IEA pairs remain single-source.

\subsection{Verification Latency}
\label{ssec:latency}

Over all \LatencyN{} answers of both live rounds, verification takes a median of \LatencyMedian\,ms per answer (95th percentile \LatencyPninetyfive\,ms, maximum \LatencyMax\,ms) with repair and the support screen on, timed cold. The fail-closed revision raised the cost, since every sentence is now also scanned for bare references, case citations and residual reference shapes, but it remains about three orders of magnitude below the 12.8\,s that graph traversal and adversarial generation add in \cite{bindra2026}.

\subsection{The Evaluation Harness as a Failure Surface}
\label{ssec:harness}

Early in the project every result was guarded only by a check of the form \texttt{python3 eval/run\_X.py >/dev/null \&\& echo PASS}, which confirms that a script did not crash and nothing about what it computed. We converted every evaluation script to return its metrics as structured values, printed the same tables from those values, and asserted explicit numeric thresholds on them, together with the invariants of Section~\ref{ssec:invariants}, checked directly: that no verification module imports an inference, learned-similarity or network library (by walking the import graph); that verification is deterministic; that no emitted answer contains an unresolvable citation under any repair and support configuration; that V1, V2 and V4 strip; and that V3 and V5 never strip. The suite now holds \TestCount{} tests.

We validated the harness by mutation (Table~\ref{tab:mutation}): seven defects, each corresponding to a claim in this paper, were injected one at a time; all seven are caught by the new assertions and none by the old check. With V2's stripping disabled, so that fabricated provisions survive into the emitted output, every evaluation script still exits zero, while the new suite fails six assertions. A further set of assertions ties the paper to the code: each takes a figure from the harness, formats it as this paper writes it, and checks that it appears in the paper's source, so a measurement that changes fails the suite until the text is updated.

\section{Discussion and Comparison with Prior Systems}

Hybrid retrieval beats both single arms on our questions, in line with \cite{bolla2026}, \cite{rustamov2025}, \cite{sun2025} (MRR@10 0.676 against 0.604 and 0.638, and 0.821 after passage reranking), but not on AILA's fact situations, where plain stemmed BM25 is our best arm and half the strength of the best 2019 run. Absolute recall sits well below 1.0, which is the empirical case for the abstention gate.

The verifier does what it promises and no more. Post-verifier resolution is 1.0 by construction, and the second live round shows how much work that is: \RtwoUngroundedPre{} of ungrounded citations resolved before verification, and the verifier removed the rest at a cost of a quarter of the sentences. What it does not promise is support, and the labelled sample shows where support comes from: grounding. \SupGroundedPrec{} of surviving grounded sentences are fully supported by what they cite; \SupUngroundedPrec{} of surviving ungrounded ones are. Reporting resolution and support separately is the point; a single ``citation accuracy'' figure would hide one inside the other.

The recodification handling matters in practice. Queries phrased around familiar IPC sections are still common, and a system that silently answers under superseded law is more dangerous than one that declines, because the wrong answer sounds plausible. On the real corpus, retrieval now puts current law first in 8 of 9 cases, and the verifier names the successor in all 9.

Nyaya-Sathi comes closest, with a database check, but relies on expert confirmation. Second, the cost: milliseconds per answer against seconds. Third, recodification: no surveyed system maps repealed IPC, CrPC or IEA sections to their successors, while ours does so for 1,084 provisions, at both retrieval and verification.

\section{Limitations and Threats to Validity}

First, and most important, the verifier guarantees that citations resolve, not that they are apposite. The labelled sample (Section~\ref{ssec:support}) measures how often a surviving citation fails to support its claim, and the advisory screen catches only part of those: topically adjacent misattribution --- citing the punishment section for a claim about the definition --- is beyond a lexical method, because adjacent provisions share vocabulary by construction (Appendix~\ref{app:dev}). Closing that gap needs semantic entailment, which we keep out of the deterministic layer because admitting a model there would forfeit the property that makes the layer trustworthy.

Second, the case-law check is closed-world and shallow. A case citation resolves only against 110 judgments or text the answer was grounded on, so a real precedent outside them is stripped (a self-composed answer citing \emph{Shreya Singhal v.\ Union of India} lost a sentence this way); it checks that the case exists, not what it held; and it covers Supreme Court reporters and ``v.''/``vs'' names only. Judgments are not yet retrieved for generation.

Third, the guarantee is bounded by recognition. The residual scan strips anything that looks like a reference and was not read, which closed every construct of the fail-closed round, but a citation that does not look like one --- ``the Arnesh Kumar judgment'', a provision named only by its subject --- is neither read nor scanned. Group references (``Part III'', ``Chapter XVI'') are deliberately not treated as provision citations.

Fourth, sample sizes are small and many query sets are ours. The gold retrieval set has 16 questions, the gate's held-out sets 8 per class, and the probe sets were written alongside the components they test. The second live round, the AILA queries and the labelled support sample are more independent, but the support labels come from one non-expert annotator.

Fifth, our corpus is bounded to ten Central Acts and 110 Supreme Court judgments. State legislation, High Court jurisprudence and multilingual queries are out of scope, and abstention on them is the intended behaviour.

Sixth, provenance. The statutory text is Indian Kanoon's, checked against the Gazette only for the three new codes; the mapping is a state police department's tables, corroborated by a second table only for BNS$\leftrightarrow$IPC; and section-to-section correspondence across the recodification is not always one-to-one.

Seventh, the confidence gate is calibrated rather than deterministic and can both refuse an answerable question and accept an unanswerable one; its figures rest on small held-out sets.

Eighth, the evaluation is incomplete against our own plan: IL-TUR and RAGAS scoring have not been run, and on AILA our retrieval underperforms the best published system.

\section{Conclusion}

Every legal RAG system in the surveyed literature controls hallucination statistically, and none reaches zero. We have argued that the residual is structural: a probabilistic mechanism cannot supply a guarantee. Is It Legal?\ makes citation resolution a property of the architecture rather than of the model, with a verifier that is deterministic, fail-closed and cheap, and that strips every citation it cannot resolve on real statutory text and on answers from three independent models. We have been careful about what that guarantee is: it covers resolution, not support; the support gap is measured on a labelled sample rather than assumed closed; and our retrieval, strong on our own questions, is mid-table on a community benchmark.

Future work includes query reduction for fact-situation retrieval and a full IL-TUR evaluation; expert-labelled support judgments and an entailment-based support check placed outside the deterministic layer; retrieving judgments for generation and checking what a cited case held, not only that it exists; traversal of a combined statute--judgment citation graph; temporal reasoning over amendment history; and multilingual queries.

\section*{Ethical Considerations}

This system is a legal research and citation tool, not a source of legal advice, and is presented to users as such. The access-to-justice case for reducing the cost of locating authority is strong, but so is the risk that a fluent system is over-relied upon by users unable to evaluate it. The abstention mechanism, the visible provenance of every claim and the prominence of the disclaimer are design responses to that risk rather than afterthoughts. Source materials are public-domain government and judicial documents; no personally identifying case data beyond what the courts have already published is introduced.

\section{Development-Corpus Experiments}
\label{app:dev}

Before the real corpus existed, each component was built and attacked on a 35-provision development corpus covering ten Central enactments and the Constitution. Its section texts are paraphrased summaries, except Article 21, which is verbatim so that the quotation check has real text to match. These measurements show whether each mechanism behaves as designed; none is a claim about legal accuracy. The fail-closed revision of Section~\ref{ssec:realverif} changed none of the figures below: these probe sets could not contain any construct it fixed, which is itself the lesson of this appendix.

\subsection{Citation Normalisation}

On 48 citation formats --- abbreviations, expanded act names, pre- and post-posed section words, sub-section and letter suffixes, the u/s shorthand, citations embedded in text --- and 8 negative strings that contain numbers but are not citations, the normaliser resolves 48/48 with 0/8 false positives. Two errors found during this evaluation shared one cause: a letter suffix greedily took the ``o'' of ``of'' in ``Article 14 of the Constitution'' (CONST\S14O) and the ``C'' of ``Cr.P.C.''. A third came from a trailing word boundary that failed after an abbreviation ending in a full stop. Citation normalisation is not a solved regex problem, and the verifier's guarantee depends on it.

\subsection{Verifier Fault Detection}

On 17 answers with known labels --- 4 clean and 13 containing an unresolved marker, a fabricated provision or a repealed reference --- the verifier detects all 13 faults with no false positives (precision and recall 1.000), keeping all of each clean answer (coverage 1.000) and 0.436 of each faulty one on average. The answers were written to contain faults the verifier is designed to catch, so the result confirms the checks are implemented correctly, not that they catch every fault.

\subsection{Quotation Verification: A Negative Result}

Character-level fuzzy matching scored a single-word, meaning-inverting substitution in an eighteen-word Article 21 quotation (``by statute'' for ``by law'') at 98.1\% partial and 95.9\% full ratio, passing every threshold in [0.80, 0.98]: a long correct prefix dominates the score. Token-level scoring rejected it at $\theta = 0.95$ (it scores 94.4\%) and admitted it at 0.90 and below. That justified $\theta = 0.95$ only for quotations shorter than twenty tokens, as Section~\ref{ssec:quotes} shows; the deployed rule is now exact.

\subsection{Support Screening and Its Failure Boundary}

The verifier checks that a citation exists, not that it supports the claim: ``Murder is punishable under Section 138 of the NI Act [1]'' passes V1--V4. V5 approximates support deterministically by IDF-weighted vocabulary overlap between the claim and the cited provision, with citation spans removed. If a provision supports a claim they should share substantive vocabulary; the converse does not hold, so the test works in one direction only. Table~\ref{tab:support} shows it separates unrelated provisions cleanly but not topically adjacent ones --- a murder claim shares vocabulary with both the punishment and the attempt provisions --- where the best attainable F1 is 0.83 at a flagging precision of 0.71.

This is a limit of vocabulary-based methods, not an implementation error: related provisions use the same words and differ in what they do with them. V5 therefore never strips (I4). It is deployed at $\tau_s = 0.20$: flagging precision stays 1.000 for $\tau_s \in [0.025, 0.225]$ and recall plateaus at 0.818 from 0.150, and 0.20 lies below the lowest score of a correct adjacent claim (0.24). Procedural claims with little vocabulary (``Charges were framed under sections 318 and 103 of the BNS'') scored zero and were falsely flagged until V5 was made to abstain below three substantive terms and to ignore claim-framing verbs (``provides'', ``charged''); after that it keeps precision 1.000 and recall 0.818.

\subsection{Evidence-Bounded Repair}

On 27 answers, repair recovers 16.7 percentage points of coverage on faulty answers (0.294 to 0.460; 0.451 to 0.580 overall) with an independent post-hoc audit passing on all 27 in both modes; nine repairs fire. Two errors were found: the first answer set contained no case that could trigger R1, so its measured gain was zero, and R2 produced ungrammatical output (``is punishable Section 318 BNS'') by dropping a leading connective. Test sets built for one behaviour can miss another.

\subsection{Cross-Reference Resolution}

On the development corpus the resolver finds 7 intra-Act edges and separates the five deliberately non-self-contained provisions (scores 0.86--1.00) from the other thirty (0.00). For five queries whose answer is such a provision, graph expansion raised Recall@1 from 0.800 to 1.000 at a candidate cost of 1.2 documents, with no change on ordinary queries. With 35 provisions, and synthetic provisions whose marginal notes leak lexical signal, this shows the mechanism works, not its effect on real retrieval.

\subsection{Parser Evasion: Twelve Defects}

The checks apply only to citations the parser extracts, so the parser was attacked directly. Three audits found twelve defects, each letting a fabricated citation through unchecked or validating a wrong one: D1, sentence splitting at legal abbreviations (``Sec.'', ``v.''); D2, enumerations (``sections 302 and 304 of the IPC'') extracting nothing; D3, unterminated quotations bypassing V4; D4, act-first enumerations (``BNS sections 318 and 7777''); D5, slash, semicolon and ``r/w'' separators; D6, markdown inside a citation; D7, zero-width characters; D8, footnote markers between section and act; D9, parenthesised section numbers; D10, a repeated section symbol in a list (``\S80, \S118''); D11, hyphenated suffixes (``Section 65-B'' truncated to a real but unrelated \S65, a false validation); and D12, an act name followed by a parenthesised section. D10--D12 were found in real model output, not by probing. After repair, a probe set of 21 concealed faults and 8 valid controls in the same syntactic forms gives precision and recall 1.000. Re-running every earlier experiment after the first fixes changed no reported number: the probe sets had, by coincidence of authorship, avoided every broken construct --- the warning the fail-closed revision of Section~\ref{ssec:realverif} finally acted on.

\subsection{Support via Cross-Reference}

Provisions that only refer elsewhere (``cognizance of any offence punishable under section 138'') carry almost no topical vocabulary, so V5 flagged correct claims cited to them: on 11 such probes its flagging precision fell to 0.500. Suppressing the flag whenever a one-hop reference supports the claim would also rescue wrong adjacent citations, so a third, advisory verdict --- support via reference --- is reported instead. This removes three of five false alarms (precision 0.667) while precision on the earlier sets stays 1.000 (recall 0.818 to 0.727).

\subsection{Calibrated Abstention on the Development Corpus}

With 16 author-written unanswerable queries split dev/test, the normalised signal made no test errors at $\tau_c = 0.305$ while raw BM25 top-1, best on dev, made two. The threshold does not transfer to the real corpus (Section~\ref{ssec:gate}), where it answers out-of-scope questions such as ``How is agricultural income taxed?''.
